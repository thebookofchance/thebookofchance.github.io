## 1. Distinct contribution

This is a new standalone essay about the gap between **decision quality** and **outcome quality**. It uses an explicitly imagined weather-warning decision to establish the problem, then distinguishes evaluating the information available beforehand from taking responsibility for consequences afterward. It does not repeat Letter 001's main focus on unseen strangers, and it leaves a more complete account of duties to strangers for the planned Letter 003.

**Core claim:** A careful choice cannot guarantee a good ending. Bad outcomes must still be taken seriously through listening, repair, accountability and learning, without treating all unforeseeable harm as proof of moral failure.

**Structure:** opening illustration; two questions we confuse; limits of good intentions; continuing responsibility; consent and imposed risk; learning from outcomes; a modest closing; reflection questions and optional practice; disclosure and references.

## 2. Alignment with the house standard

- Uses Commonwealth spelling and the sober, open tone of H; no prophecy, miracles, promises of control or exclusion.
- Grounds questions in imagined situations, and identifies both fictional illustrations in the closing note.
- Keeps reader practice voluntary and private; no demand for a declaration, public sharing, membership or payment.
- Avoids telling people facing hardship that they caused it, or that harm is always meaningful or deserved.
- Recognises the different responsibility of individuals and organisations and the additional justification needed when risks are imposed on others.
- Connects explicitly to **Fortune and Hardship**, **Action Within Uncertainty** and **Reflection and Growth**, without changing the foundational manuscript.
- Acknowledges **moral luck** as a pre-existing philosophical problem. The essay does not claim that this debate began with Confluent Humanism.

## 3. Sources and checking

The discussion of moral luck draws on introductory reference accounts of debates developed by Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams:

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, [Moral Luck](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-luck/), accessed 22 November 2026.
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, [Moral Decision-Making Under Uncertainty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-decision-uncertainty/), accessed 22 November 2026.

These links are included in the actual Letter. The text does not quote those sources or present an empirical study. The argument and scenes are original philosophical writing, not verified records or claims of scientific proof.
