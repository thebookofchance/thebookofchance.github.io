Letters from H · No. 03

What Do We Owe to Strangers?

We cannot answer every need. But we need not know someone to recognise that they matter.

By H · Prepared 22 November 2026 · Editorial candidate 1.0-RC1

Imagine a small decision at a supermarket checkout. A person ahead of you puts an item back after counting the money in their hand. You could offer to pay the difference. You do not know whether the item matters greatly to them, whether they would welcome help, or whether an offer made in front of other customers might embarrass them. You also know what remains in your own account. Some of that money is already spoken for.

No one has asked you to decide what a good person would do. Yet the question arrives anyway.

We are accustomed to recognising duties that come with a name. We have promises to keep, people who depend on us, relationships that deserve our time. A stranger brings none of these familiar claims. We may never see them again. Why should their difficulty matter to us? And if it does, where does our responsibility end?

The answer cannot be that we must solve every need we encounter. Neither can it be that a person matters only after we know their name.

A claim before an introduction

A stranger is not someone outside the circle of human concern. They are someone about whom we know very little.

That difference matters. We cannot know what another person wants merely by noticing that they are struggling. We may misread a situation. We may offer the wrong thing. But uncertainty about what would help is not uncertainty about whether the person deserves respect.

Some obligations arise before friendship. We ought not to deceive people simply because we have no relationship with them. We should not endanger them carelessly, exploit their lack of knowledge, or treat their need as an opportunity to exercise power. These restraints do not become weaker because the person affected will never be able to thank us or hold us to account.

There are also occasions when doing nothing seems difficult to justify. If I can warn someone about an immediate danger at almost no cost or risk, the absence of an introduction is a poor reason to stay silent. A person's vulnerability can give us a reason to respond even when there is no agreement between us.

But a reason is not always a command with an obvious answer. Some emergencies require training. Intervening can worsen a situation. A person may refuse assistance. Asking for qualified help can be wiser than becoming the centre of a crisis we do not understand.

The first question, then, is not How much goodness do I owe? It is What kind of claim does this situation place upon me, and what am I actually able to do?

The people we have promised to

Suppose you have promised to collect a family member from an appointment. On the way, you notice someone who appears lost and asks for directions. You could stop for a moment. You could also stay for half an hour, accompany them across town and miss the person who is relying on you.

It would be strange to say that because both people have equal dignity, they must receive equal amounts of your time.

A promise creates a particular responsibility. So does caring for a child, supporting a dependent person, or taking on a role in which others have reasonably placed their trust. These obligations are not proof that some lives are worth more than others. They describe relationships in which our choices have become especially important to particular people.

We have reason to honour those ties. They make dependable care possible. If everyone abandoned existing commitments whenever an unfamiliar need appeared, the world would not become more compassionate. It would become less reliable.

Yet our close relationships cannot excuse everything we do for their benefit. Loving our own family does not entitle us to cheat someone else's. Loyalty to a friend cannot justify ignoring a serious injury we could reasonably prevent. A community that reserves every opportunity for insiders may be affectionate within its walls and profoundly unfair beyond them.

There is a tension here that no simple formula removes: people deserve equal moral regard, but our responsibilities to them are not always identical. To acknowledge the tension is more honest than pretending either that private loyalties settle every question or that they have no legitimate place in an ethical life.

What a need may ask of us

We often encounter need at an uneven distance. A person beside us may require a small act of immediate help. Someone far away may face a much greater danger. Another person might need something we cannot supply at all.

Distance can change what we know and what we can do. It does not, by itself, change what a life is worth.

In deciding how to respond, several considerations deserve attention. How serious is the need? How likely is this particular action to help? What will it cost, and who will carry that cost? Are we unusually well placed to act? Has help been requested or welcomed? Is another person, organisation or public service better equipped to respond?

These are questions, not a calculation of anyone's worth. They help us distinguish an urgent duty from a kind offer, and a kind offer from an intervention that might do harm.

There is a powerful objection to setting limits. If we can ease suffering and choose not to, aren't we placing our comfort above another person's need? Sometimes we are. We should be willing to examine that possibility, particularly when we have considerable resources and the sacrifice asked of us is slight.

But there is a second danger. If every need becomes an unlimited demand upon whoever notices it, those with the least security may be asked to surrender what they cannot spare. A person who is exhausted, unwell, caring for others or struggling to meet essential costs cannot be judged by the same practical expectations as a well-resourced institution. Nor should any individual be expected to spend every waking hour proving that strangers matter.

Rest, friendship, privacy and ordinary joy are not thefts from the people we have not helped. They are parts of a human life worth protecting. An ethic that makes care impossible to sustain may ultimately offer less care, not more.

The boundary between what we must do and what would be generous is contested. We should not draw it only where our comfort begins. We should also resist allowing guilt, public attention or someone else's demands to draw it for us.

Help without possession

Return to the person at the checkout. Perhaps an offer to cover the difference would be welcome. Perhaps it would be unwelcome, especially if made loudly or with the expectation of gratitude. You might ask quietly, accept the answer, and allow the moment to end without claiming a story about yourself.

Help does not grant ownership of the person helped.

That principle is easy to affirm in ordinary language and harder to honour when there is a difference in power. A wealthy donor may want to choose how a recipient lives. A volunteer may assume that being useful gives them access to private information. A group might begin to treat an act of generosity as proof that someone deserves influence over everyone else.

We should be wary of these exchanges. A person's need is not consent to scrutiny, publicity, religious instruction or membership. Assistance should never be made conditional on praising the giver or accepting a philosophy.

Sometimes consent is complicated: a person may not be able to speak for themselves, or the circumstances may require urgent action. Then our obligation includes humility about our knowledge, attention to appropriate safeguards, and a willingness to defer to people with relevant expertise and responsibility.

This also means accepting that an offer may be declined. A refusal does not cancel the person's dignity, and it need not become an insult to the giver. Respect includes room for another person to decide what happens next.

When a responsibility belongs to more than one person

Individual generosity matters. It can relieve a particular difficulty at precisely the moment it is felt. But some needs are repeated so often that a private favour cannot be a satisfactory answer.

If a public building has a staircase that excludes many visitors, it is good to help someone find another entrance. It is better still to ask why the entrance remains inaccessible and who is responsible for changing it. If someone must repeatedly ask strangers for help navigating an essential service, the service itself may need to change.

The burden should not rest exclusively with the person who happened to notice the problem. Institutions with resources, authority and public responsibilities can owe duties that no passer-by could fulfil alone. Fair rules, decent working conditions, accessible services and accountable decisions make ordinary care less dependent on a lucky encounter.

Nor can charitable giving replace questions of justice. A donation may help someone today while leaving intact the policy or arrangement that makes the same help necessary tomorrow. There is room for both immediate assistance and the patient work of changing conditions. Neither should be used to dismiss the other.

This is one reason a future Fellowship ought to be careful about how it speaks of giving. Voluntary support may enable worthwhile projects, but no amount donated should purchase status or influence. A campaign should explain its purpose, the limits of what it can accomplish, and how decisions and spending will be accounted for. If those arrangements are not yet in place, the honest action is to build them before asking people for money.

We cannot make kindness into a substitute for trustworthy public systems. We can, sometimes, make those systems more trustworthy together.

The reach of an ordinary life

What, then, do we owe to strangers?

We owe them at least the regard we hope not to lose when we ourselves become unfamiliar: freedom from exploitation, attention to foreseeable harm, and recognition that their choices and boundaries matter. In some situations, we owe reasonable assistance. In others, our best contribution may be to support a fairer arrangement rather than improvise a private rescue.

We do not owe every person the same relationship. We cannot offer unlimited time, money or knowledge. We may disagree, withdraw from a request, or acknowledge that someone else is better placed to help. None of these decisions needs to be a declaration that the other person's life matters less.

The question is not finally answered by an amount. It remains a judgement we must make with imperfect information and with other legitimate claims upon us.

The person at the checkout may be helped. They may decline. You may discover that you can spare nothing safely. A decent response is not guaranteed by a generous intention, and a decent person need not be the hero of the scene.

Perhaps the smallest change is this: the boundary of our concern need not be the boundary of our acquaintance.

We can keep faith with those close to us, make room for someone unknown, and work toward conditions in which fewer people must depend on either luck or a stranger's kindness to be treated fairly.

For reflection

  • Think of a time when a stranger's need conflicted with a promise, a personal limit or another responsibility. What made the decision difficult, and what would a fair account of both people require?
  • Where might a repeated need call for a better shared system rather than another act of private generosity? Who should have a voice in deciding what changes?

An optional practice: Notice one modest situation in which you could be useful to someone you do not know. Before acting, consider whether help is wanted, whether it is within your means and whether a more appropriate person or service should be involved. Offer what is reasonable, if anything, without seeking public credit. You need not record the other person's story or report your action to anyone.

A note on this letter

The supermarket and directions scenes are imagined illustrations, not reports of particular events. This Letter extends questions raised in The Book of Chance, especially Shared Fortune, Fellowship and Stewardship. It follows On the People We Never Meet by asking not merely how lives are connected, but how much those connections can reasonably ask of us. It differs from When Good Choices Lead to Bad Outcomes, which centres on judging decisions and responding to their consequences.

Questions about duties to strangers, special obligations, beneficence, justice and the moral limits of sacrifice are well-established areas of philosophical disagreement. Further reading includes the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on Special Obligations, The Principle of Beneficence in Applied Ethics and Moral Demands and Permissions. These sources contextualise the question; this Letter does not claim to resolve the debate.

This is a philosophical invitation, not a demand to donate, join a community or disclose private experiences. Readers are welcome to disagree.
