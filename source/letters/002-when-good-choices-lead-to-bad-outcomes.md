# When Good Choices Lead to Bad Outcomes

*Letters from H · No. 02*

Imagine a small community preparing an outdoor gathering. By morning, the weather forecast carries a serious warning. The organisers have checked the information, spoken with those responsible for safety and considered the people who would have to travel. They decide to postpone.

The afternoon turns out calm. The sun appears. Some people are disappointed, and a few say the organisers were foolish to cancel. Money has been spent. A volunteer had arranged time away from work. Looking back at the clear sky, the decision seems unnecessary.

But the sky that appeared was not the only sky that might have appeared.

Suppose instead that the warning had been followed by dangerous winds. The same decision would be praised as sensible. Nothing about the organisers' information or care at the moment of choice would have changed. Only the weather—and the story told afterward—would be different.

This is an imagined situation, but the problem is familiar. We decide without knowing everything that will follow. Then, once an outcome is visible, we are tempted to treat it as though it had been inevitable. What does that temptation do to our judgement of ourselves and others?

## Two questions we tend to confuse

When something goes wrong, it is natural to ask, *Was this a bad decision?* Often what we first mean is that the result was painful, disappointing or costly. That is a real concern. Someone may have lost an opportunity. Someone may have been hurt. The damage cannot be made harmless by describing the intentions behind it.

But there is another question: *Was the choice reasonable when it was made?*

These questions are related, but they are not identical. A person can act carelessly and escape harm by good fortune. Another can act carefully and encounter a consequence that could not reasonably have been predicted. To judge only by the visible ending is to overlook what either person was actually choosing between.

The distinction helps us locate responsibility more accurately: first in the care a person took when choosing, then in how they respond to the result.

Before a decision, we have the information available to us, the people we can consult, the time we have, the options we can recognise and the risks we can reasonably anticipate. Afterward, we know at least one thing we did not know before: what happened. It is tempting to carry that knowledge backward and imagine that it was obvious all along.

We can resist that temptation by asking what a careful person, with the same relevant information and genuine constraints, could have understood at the time. Not what a perfect person would have known. Not what someone says now, standing safely on the other side of the event.

## Intention is not enough

There is a danger in taking this distinction too far. Someone might say, 'I meant well,' and expect the conversation to end there. Yet a sincere wish to help does not automatically make an action careful, fair or welcome.

Imagine that a person offers a friend a job opportunity in another city. The friend considers it, accepts and later finds that the workplace is unsafe. The offer may have been made with generosity. It might also have rested on information that the person sharing it could have checked. If the warning signs were evident and ignored, good intentions do not settle the matter.

What matters includes the care taken before acting: whether important risks were investigated, whether claims were honest, whether consent was meaningful, whether those affected had room to decline and whether there were safer alternatives. Power matters too. A manager making a decision for a whole team owes more preparation than a friend offering a tentative suggestion over coffee.

Reasonable care grows with the consequences a choice may impose on others. It asks for preparation proportionate to the risk, rather than impossible foresight.

Sometimes there is no good option. A parent may have to choose between insecure work and an uncertain relocation. A caregiver may face incompatible needs. Someone with little money may be unable to purchase the safer option available to a wealthier person. Calling these decisions 'choices' must not erase the narrowness of the paths offered.

A philosophy of responsibility that ignores circumstances can become another way of blaming people for the hardships they inherit.

## What follows still matters

Now imagine that the organisers of the cancelled gathering discover that postponement caused a serious difficulty for one attendee. Perhaps travel costs cannot be recovered. Perhaps a person who needed access to the gathering will not be able to attend the replacement date.

The original choice may still have been reasonable. That does not make the difficulty unreal.

There are several kinds of response. The organisers can listen rather than defend themselves immediately. They can explain the information on which the decision was based without pretending it guarantees correctness. They can ask whether an adjustment or practical remedy is possible. They can record what they learned so the next decision is better informed.

An apology can acknowledge harm without falsely claiming that all harm was foreseeable. Where a person or organisation owes a remedy, that obligation should not depend solely on proving bad intentions. And where no remedy is possible, people affected still deserve to be heard.

We sometimes treat responsibility as a verdict pronounced on character: guilty or innocent, wise or foolish, good or bad. But it can also be a practice that continues after a choice: examining what followed, repairing what we can, and changing a procedure when new evidence arrives. A cancelled gathering might be followed by a shared transport arrangement or an accessible alternative date. Such a response cannot erase disappointment. It can show people that their difficulty has been taken seriously.

## Who carries the risk?

A decision can seem sensible to the person making it while asking someone else to bear most of the danger. This is easy to miss when we speak only of the average likelihood of a favourable result.

A small probability of harm may be tolerable when a person understands it and freely accepts it for themselves. The same risk may require greater justification when it is imposed on someone who did not consent, especially when the harm would be severe or hard to reverse.

This is why the question *What is most likely to work?* cannot be our only guide. We must also ask *For whom? Who has a say? Who will carry the cost if we are wrong?* The answer may lead us to postpone, redesign, consult, share a burden or leave someone the freedom to refuse.

No rule will settle every difficult case. Sometimes protecting one person places a burden on another. Sometimes delay carries a risk of its own. Even a carefully reached agreement cannot remove all uncertainty.

But the presence of uncertainty makes honest deliberation more important, not less. Our hope for a good result must not become permission to gamble casually with another person's life.

## Learning without inventing certainty

It is possible to learn from a bad result without deciding that the original choice must have been bad. We can ask whether a warning was missed, whether different people should have been consulted, whether a procedure worked as intended, or whether the outcome revealed a risk no one had yet recognised.

We can also learn from a good result without assuming it proves our wisdom. A reckless shortcut might succeed once. Luck can hide a weakness just as it can expose one.

This requires a particular kind of honesty. We must be able to say, 'I chose as carefully as I could, and something painful followed.' We must also be able to say, 'I was fortunate this time, but I should not make the same decision again.' Neither statement is easy when reputation, guilt or pride is involved.

The philosopher Thomas Nagel and the philosopher Bernard Williams helped frame a related problem often called *moral luck*: outcomes and circumstances beyond our control can shape how our conduct is judged. Their work does not offer a simple test for every decision. It helps explain why the gap between what we control and what we are praised or blamed for can be so troubling.

Taking chance seriously asks for a more careful account of what a choice can accomplish. Deliberation matters because consequences matter, including those we could not guarantee or foresee.

## The choice we can make now

A person living through a painful result rarely needs a philosophical lecture about uncertainty. They may need safety, time, an honest account of what happened or someone willing to help repair it. Reflection should not be used to quiet their anger or hurry their grief.

For the person who made the decision, there may be a different task: to resist both easy self-excuse and endless self-punishment. One asks too little of us; the other can leave us unable to respond to what is still possible.

Between them is a harder practice. Look truthfully at the information you had. Look again at the consequences. Listen where others were affected. Make amends where you can. Change what you have reason to change. Allow that some losses are real even when no one chose them.

Return to the gathering that was postponed. The organisers do not earn wisdom because the storm comes, nor become fools because it does not. Their decision must be examined in the light in which it was made, and its effects in the light that followed.

We can prepare with care, respond with generosity when plans fail, and let what we learn improve the next decision. An imperfect result may still be followed by a useful act of solidarity.

A good outcome is something to welcome. A good decision is something we must learn to make without the promise of one.

### For reflection

- Think of a decision whose outcome changed how you judged it. What could reasonably have been known beforehand, and what became clear only later?
- If someone was affected by a decision you made, what might they need from you now—an explanation, a remedy, a changed practice, or simply the space to be heard?

**An optional practice:** Before one modest decision this week, write down the information you are using, one uncertainty you cannot remove, and who might carry the consequences. Afterward, revisit those notes. Judge the quality of your reasoning separately from the result, and notice anything that merits repair or a change of approach. You need not share your notes with anyone.

### A note on this letter

The opening gathering and the employment example are imagined illustrations, not reported events. This Letter develops themes from *The Book of Chance*, particularly **Fortune and Hardship**, **Action Within Uncertainty** and **Reflection and Growth**. It does not claim that every bad outcome was unavoidable, that well-meant harm can always be excused, or that reasonable decisions remove duties of care, remedy or accountability.

The discussion of *moral luck* acknowledges an established philosophical debate rather than presenting that question as an invention of Confluent Humanism. For an introduction to the debate and further references, see the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Moral Luck](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-luck/). For the different moral questions raised by decisions under uncertainty, see its [entry on Moral Decision-Making Under Uncertainty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-decision-uncertainty/).

The argument is offered for examination, not as a doctrine readers must accept. It requires neither membership nor financial support.
