# On the People We Never Meet

*Letters from H · No. 01*

There is a handrail beside a steep flight of steps in a public building. Most people who use it do not think about the person who requested it, the person who measured its height, or the worker who fixed it to the wall. Some may never need it. Others arrive on a difficult morning and find something steady to hold.

The people involved in making that rail may never know whose fall it prevented, whose climb it made possible, or whether it helped at all. Their work belongs to the lives of strangers.

We often imagine that our influence is strongest where we can see it. We care for a friend, encourage a child, help someone find an answer. There is a face to remember and, sometimes, a reply. We know where to direct our gratitude and whom to ask whether our help was welcome.

But our lives also meet without our ever standing in the same room. Much of what sustains us comes from people whose names we will never learn. Much of what we pass on reaches people we cannot picture. If we take that seriously, what changes about the way we choose?

## The work that has no witness

Consider the ordinary arrangements of a town. Drinking water reaches a tap. A bus has a route and a timetable. A public library keeps its doors open. Someone checks a bridge. Someone corrects an error in a guide that others depend upon.

These are not all acts of generosity. People may have been paid, required by law, assigned a shift, or simply doing a job they did not especially enjoy. The reasons matter. Fair wages and safe working conditions matter. Calling every necessary task a gift would make it too easy to overlook the person doing it.

Yet the effect remains: lives are made more or less possible through work that rarely enters anyone's personal story. I can walk across a bridge without meeting the person who inspected it. I can rely on instructions improved by a stranger's patience. I can benefit from a public decision made years before I arrived.

There is something humbling in this. We are more dependent than the stories of individual achievement tend to suggest. That dependency does not erase effort or make achievement unreal. It gives effort a setting. A person who finishes a difficult course has done something substantial. They may also have relied on safe roads, working electricity, someone who maintained the classroom, and more than one person willing to teach.

To notice that is not to diminish anyone. It is to tell a fuller story.

## When influence travels without permission

Our connections to strangers are not always beneficial. A careless decision can make someone's work harder. A policy written at a distance can close a door. A habit that seems insignificant in one household may become consequential when repeated by many.

An invisible effect is not automatically a good one.

There is also danger in treating every possible consequence as our personal responsibility. None of us can trace the whole path of a purchase, a sentence, a journey or a choice. We do not control all the systems in which we take part. To insist that a decent person foresee everything would turn ethical life into an impossible burden, and perhaps a reason to do nothing at all.

A more workable standard asks what we could reasonably have known, what power we actually held, what harms were foreseeable, and what better alternatives were available. The person with authority to alter a dangerous design has a different responsibility from the person who discovers that design while trying to get home. A large institution can investigate consequences that no individual customer can reliably uncover.

This is one reason that kindness, while essential, is insufficient by itself. Helping a stranger cross a difficult street matters. So does asking why the crossing is dangerous, and whether it can be made safer for everyone who comes afterward.

## A letter that arrives too late

Imagine a person who finds an old note tucked into the back of a borrowed book. The note is brief: a previous reader has listed three useful sources on a difficult subject. There is no name, no request for thanks and no way to know whether the note was intended for anyone else.

The new reader follows one of the references and finds the explanation they needed. The note has done something useful, even though its writer will never hear about it.

That is a small imagined story, not evidence of a hidden law that good deeds return to their authors. They often do not. A note can be lost. Advice can mislead. An introduction may be unwelcome. The person helped may never discover that help existed.

Still, we can recognise something worth doing: leaving accurate information where it can be found, improving a tool other people use, making a space easier to enter, or correcting a mistake before it is passed along. There is a kind of care that does not require an audience.

The absence of thanks does not make care worthless. Neither does it make every well-intended action wise. We owe strangers attention to the likely effects of what we leave behind, not merely confidence in our motives.

## The stranger is not an imagined version of ourselves

It is tempting to think of an unknown person as someone just like us, living farther away or later in time. Sometimes that helps us extend concern beyond our familiar circle. But it can also mislead us.

The person using that handrail might have needs we have never considered. The person receiving our advice may face risks we do not share. A community may have its own understanding of what help would be useful, what counts as respect, or which problem deserves attention first.

Good intentions do not give us the right to decide for people we have not consulted.

When possible, we should listen to those affected, involve them in decisions and remain willing to revise our plans. When direct consultation is impossible—because the people live far away, have not yet been born, or cannot speak for themselves—we should be more modest, not more certain. We can draw on reliable evidence, protect room for future choices and avoid making irreversible decisions merely because those who bear the cost cannot object today.

This is not an argument for saving the whole world in a single gesture. It is an argument against mistaking distance for absence.

## The people who have not arrived

Some strangers will live after us.

A tree planted in the right place may offer shade long after its planter has moved away. A damaged waterway may outlast the people who profited from its neglect. A well-maintained record can save future workers from repeating an error. A badly designed institution can hand them a problem without leaving the means to repair it.

We cannot ask future generations to approve our choices. They will inherit something from us nevertheless. We also cannot know precisely which futures they will value or what tools they will have. The task, then, is not to compose their lives in advance. It is to avoid needlessly closing their options.

This connects to a conviction running through *The Book of Chance*: we begin among conditions others have helped make. Some support us; some constrain us. We then participate, however modestly, in making conditions for people after us.

The future may be open, but openness does not cancel consequences. A choice can narrow what someone else can reasonably attempt even if no particular outcome was ever guaranteed. Conversely, care can preserve a possibility without ensuring that anyone uses it.

It is enough to matter that the possibility remains.

## What we can reasonably give

There is a limit to every person's time, money and attention. If our responsibilities expanded to include every stranger in the world, all at once and without distinction, few of us could live under their weight. We also have duties to the people close to us and to our own wellbeing. These are not moral defects to be overcome.

We can begin more simply.

Where our work affects others, we can try to do it carefully. Where we possess useful knowledge, we can share it accurately and without claiming more certainty than we have. Where a rule excludes people unnecessarily, we can ask whether it should change. Where we have the capacity to support an accountable public or community effort, we can consider doing so. Where we are unable to help, we need not manufacture guilt to prove that we care.

Not every useful contribution costs money. Not every contribution needs to be public. And a person who is exhausted, unwell, unsafe or struggling to meet basic needs has not failed because their attention must turn inward for a time.

The measure is not how many invisible lives we can claim to have improved. That would turn people we have never met into evidence for our own virtue. We can be responsible for the care of an action without being entitled to a satisfying story about its effects.

## A place to hold

Return to the handrail.

Someone thought a passage could be safer. Someone listened, or perhaps did not listen soon enough. Someone made a decision and others carried it out. The rail might have been designed badly and needed adjustment. It might remain unused for days and prove essential on an ordinary afternoon.

No single person can claim all the good it may do. Nor can we know every person who will touch it.

There will be strangers in our lives whom we never meet, and others who will live in places or years we will never see. We should not pretend to know them. We can still take their existence seriously.

Perhaps that begins with a modest question: *What am I helping to leave in place for someone whose name I will never know?*

### For reflection

- Can you name something useful in your life that was made possible by people you never met? What would it mean to acknowledge it without romanticising the work involved?
- Is there a decision within your actual influence that could make life easier, safer or more open for an unknown person?

**An optional practice:** Choose one ordinary system, shared resource or piece of information you help maintain. Ask whether a small, realistic improvement would benefit its next user. If others are affected, seek their input before changing it.

### A note on this letter

This letter extends themes in *The Book of Chance*, especially **Shared Fortune**, **The Living World** and **Stewardship**. The handrail and found note are illustrative scenes; they are not reports of verified events. The reflection is an ethical argument offered for consideration, not a promise that good intentions will produce good outcomes. Readers may disagree without belonging to any organisation.
