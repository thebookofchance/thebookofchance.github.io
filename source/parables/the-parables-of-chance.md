## A Note on This Edition

These twelve stories are fiction. Their characters, settings and events have been invented for this collection. Although some details resemble ordinary circumstances, none is presented as a verified case study, testimony or account of a Fellowship programme.

*The Parables of Chance* is a literary companion to *The Book of Chance*. The stories are informed by questions associated with Confluent Humanism: the circumstances we inherit, the limits of prediction, the consequences of choice, and the ways our lives affect others. They do not attempt to prove a metaphysical theory of chance. No ending offers a guaranteed moral reward. Readers may disagree about a character's choices—or decide that the important question is one the story does not answer.

H is a pseudonymous authorial byline, not a source of special authority. The collection was developed with AI assistance under human direction and has been approved by H for digital publication. Independent editorial endorsement has not been claimed. The Fellowship of Chance is an organisation in development; no membership programme, hardship fund or mentoring service is implied to exist by these fictional stories. ## Before the Stories

A parable is sometimes expected to behave like a locked box: the reader searches for a key, opens it and discovers the lesson placed inside. These stories were written with a different hope.

A person may act carefully and still be wrong. A useful gesture may come too late. A refusal may be the most generous thing someone can offer. What seems fortunate to one person may be difficult for another. Even when people want the same good thing, they may disagree about how to reach it.

There are no points for finding the 'correct' interpretation. Some stories end before the consequences can be known. Others end after the event that initially seemed most important. The characters have lives beyond the questions they help us notice.

Read one story, or several. Leave the book closed for a while if a subject is heavy. It is also possible to enjoy the voices, places and small absurdities without turning every detail into a symbol.

# Part I — What Arrives Unasked

*No one begins at the same address. Nor do we always arrive where we intended.*

## 1. The Wrong Address

The parcel was too heavy for the size of it, which was the first reason Iona remembered it.

The second was the handwriting.

**For my father, when he is ready**, said the label, followed by a street name in careful blue ink and a number that had never existed.

Iona had worked for the delivery firm long enough to know the kind of trouble caused by numbers that did not exist. A machine could decide a parcel belonged on the next street, or nowhere. By the time an actual person examined it, a birthday had passed or a buyer had asked for their money back. On a Thursday in June, she was that person.

The postcode was correct. There were forty-eight houses on the street, but no number 49. At number 47 lived a woman who had once run a bakery and now kept three enormous pots of rosemary. At 51, across a narrow lane that officially belonged to another road, lived a family of six.

Iona could have sent the parcel back. She had three trolley loads left to sort and an appointment with her own father on Friday. Instead she carried the box to number 47.

Mrs Vale was on the doorstep feeding a cracker to a bird with one foot.

“Forty-nine?” she said. “That would be the old workshop.”

“Do you know who lives there now?”

“Nobody. They pulled it down after the fire. What’s in the box?”

“I can't tell you.”

“You don't know, then.”

“I know it's not mine.”

Mrs Vale considered this, which Iona found more irritating than a simple apology. Then she pointed down the lane.

“The workshop belonged to Mr Reeve. His daughter's a painter. She moved away after the funeral, but the boy who rented the flat above the shop still has her number. Might do.”

Iona carried the parcel another three streets. The shop was closed for repairs, and the handwritten notice on the window said the tenant had moved to the coast. By the time she returned to the depot, the label had begun to peel off in the rain. She placed the box in the returns area with a note that it might belong to the family of a former resident.

At home she found a message from her father. He had cancelled their visit because, as he put it, “the sink's doing its best impression of a waterfall.” Iona phoned him. They spoke for nine minutes about pipes and whether the bus company had changed the route again. Not once did he ask her to come anyway.

The parcel remained on the shelf for eleven days.

On the twelfth, Mrs Vale appeared at the depot carrying a small paper bag of rosemary biscuits. She had found a number for Mr Reeve's daughter, Elise. Iona wanted to say that the depot was not a place where customers could investigate one another's belongings. She was tired, and the woman had crossed town in the rain.

“The daughter can contact us directly,” Iona said. “We're not authorised to release it to somebody else.”

Mrs Vale put the biscuits on the counter, looked at Iona's face, and took them back.

“Oh,” she said. “I thought this was my helpful moment.”

“That may still be true.”

Mrs Vale laughed, not warmly. She wrote the number on a scrap of paper and left it behind.

Elise called that afternoon. Mrs Vale had found Elise through people she knew, not through depot records. After the depot confirmed Elise's posting receipt and details as sender, she explained that she had sent the parcel herself months earlier, when the workshop address was already obsolete. It contained a cast-iron vice from the workshop, wrapped with a drawing her father had made while teaching her to use it. She had addressed it to him because she had not been ready to say he was dead. She had posted it from an old studio, then moved again. The forwarding instructions had expired.

“I don't know why I did that,” she said.

Iona had no sensible reply. She gave Elise the available options for retrieving it and told her how long the depot could keep it.

“Can you hold it until next Tuesday?”

“Yes.”

“Thank you. I haven't told my brother I've sent it. He thinks I threw everything away.”

On Tuesday, Elise arrived with a bicycle helmet in her hands and a face that made Iona think of someone examining an unfamiliar house from the outside. Iona asked for the required identification and lifted the box onto the counter.

Elise held it without opening it.

“Did anyone else see it?”

“Only the label.”

“Good.”

She wheeled it away on her bicycle, balanced across the pannier with a length of cord. At the corner she stopped, walked back and asked for Mrs Vale's address. Iona told her she could pass on a message but not another customer's details.

“Tell her I got it,” Elise said. “And tell her I'm glad it didn't go to the wrong person.”

Two weeks later a postcard arrived for the depot staff. It showed a bad drawing of a vice gripping a sprig of rosemary. On the back, Elise had written that she and her brother had argued over the box. They had both said things they would have preferred not to say. Then they had found a little star scratched into the metal, a mark neither remembered making. They were still discussing what to do with it.

There was no thank-you beyond that. Iona pinned the card to a corkboard beside an instruction about damaged barcodes.

That Friday, she visited her father after all. The sink was fixed. He made tea too weak and asked whether her work was still full of stupid labels.

“Mostly,” she said.

She almost told him about the parcel. Instead she asked which things in his shed mattered to him and which were merely things he had never got round to throwing out. He looked offended at first. Then he went to the shed and returned with a rusted square and a folding ruler, and the afternoon took a different shape.

Her father returned the folding ruler to the shelf and turned the rusted square in his hands.

“Your granddad made this,” he said. “He was terrible at measuring.”

Iona stayed for another cup of tea. The square remained on the table between them.


## 2. Two Flood Marks

When the water withdrew, it left two brown lines on the front of the houses.

At number 12 the line ran just below the sitting-room windowsill. At number 14 it rose as high as the second row of bricks. From the road the difference looked small. Inside the houses, it was the difference between cleaning a floor and removing every lower kitchen cupboard.

Anika stood outside number 14 with a rubbish bag in each hand. The bags contained the contents of her youngest son's school portfolio: drawings grown soft, a certificate with the signature dissolved, a photograph in which the smiling faces had slipped off the paper. They had spent the night on the upper floor of her sister's house, seven people among mattresses and a single kettle.

Across the hedge, Peter was hosing silt from his garage.

“Insurance assessor's coming Tuesday,” he called. “Have you been in touch with yours?”

Anika said she rented. The landlord had promised to arrange an inspection, but the only call she had received was from a cleaning company asking whether she had moved the furniture.

Peter turned off the hose. He had spoken too quickly, as he sometimes did when he was frightened. He was seventy-two and lived alone. He had a savings account, a spare bedroom, a house he had maintained for thirty years, and a fear that his insurance would find a reason not to pay. None of those facts told him what to say next.

“Can I do anything?”

“Do you have somewhere dry to put books?”

He looked at the garage. Its shelves were still intact.

“Yes.”

For the next hour he helped carry plastic tubs across the hedge. Anika's son Tomas labelled them with black marker: SCHOOL, KITCHEN, WINTER, KEEP DRY. He made a separate tub marked POSSIBLY DRY, which Peter regarded as a considerable improvement in the language of storage.

After lunch, a van arrived on Peter's side of the road. Two workers in clean boots measured the damage. On Anika's side, a neighbour dropped off a packet of disposable gloves. The landlord's cleaning company did not come.

By evening the street's small group chat had become a jumble of offers and declarations. Someone said everyone should simply work together. Someone else said the council had failed them. A third person shared a video that accused the residents of building in the wrong place. Anika muted the notifications.

On Wednesday Peter offered his spare room to the family.

Anika thanked him and refused. “It's kind,” she said. “But four children and school mornings in your house won't be kind to either of us.”

He was disappointed, though he knew she was right. The next day he asked again in a different form.

“I can lend you the garage shelves for as long as they're useful. No need to tell me why you come and go.”

“That we can manage.”

They agreed on a key and what hours were reasonable. Tomas made a sign saying PETER'S LIBRARY and taped it inside the garage. Under the title he drew four boxes, each with its own borrowed books.

The water had carried a lot away but left some absurd things untouched. Anika found a packet of glitter that had survived in a biscuit tin. Peter discovered his late wife's holiday postcards floating in a drawer with the writing dry. People came to admire both miracles, as if the flood had selected its survivors for a reason.

“It's just where they were,” Anika said when someone called the glitter lucky. “The certificates weren't in a tin.”

After three weeks she had seen enough charity photographs to dislike the sound of a camera shutter. Volunteers arrived with boxes of donated clothes that did not fit anyone in the house. A local business offered a television and asked whether the family would pose beside it. She said no. Another family took it.

When a third van pulled up that afternoon, Peter offered to take the boxes before Anika had even seen what was inside. She stopped him.

“First ask whether we need them,” she said.

He went back to the van, embarrassed, and asked. The driver was carrying four bags of children's coats. None was the right size.

On a Saturday, Anika joined six neighbours at the school to discuss the flood. The room smelt of wet coats. A council officer explained the possible timetable for drain repairs. He would not promise that the next storm would be different. The landlord did not attend. Peter sat near the back and took notes. He had come expecting a discussion about sandbags.

Anika asked how people renting damaged houses would be notified if inspections were delayed. The officer answered a different question. She asked again. A woman from the next street said her elderly father had received only a letter he could not read. Tomas, sitting under the table with a puzzle, looked up when he heard his mother's voice becoming steady.

By the end of the meeting there was a short list of questions the officer agreed to answer in writing. Anika volunteered to share that list with the tenants on the street, provided they wanted it. Peter said he could copy it for neighbours who did not use a phone.

“People will think you're running the whole thing now,” someone warned.

“Then we should put three names on it,” Anika said.

Two months later the line on number 12 was hidden by new paint. On number 14 it remained visible behind a stripped kitchen. Anika had still not received a reliable move-back date. The children were sharing a room at her sister's house. Peter's garage had become crowded with dry boxes and one small table where Tomas did his homework when the family visited.

One evening Peter asked if Anika would like the flood mark painted over. He could do it once the wall was repaired.

“Not until we know what caused it,” she said. “After that, maybe.”

The next morning Tomas measured the mark from the floor with a ruler. He wrote the number in a school exercise book, carefully, beside a drawing of two neighbouring houses. He drew the marks at different heights.

Peter wanted to tell him that next time they might be prepared. Anika wanted to believe there would be no next time. Neither spoke.

Tomas turned the notebook sideways so that the two marks stood beside each other like rungs of a ladder. When Anika said it was time to go, he tucked the book beneath his coat.


## 3. The Day the Signal Failed

The town called the interruption the Great Silence, though in fact it lasted less than five hours and was full of noise.

At eight minutes past nine the mobile network failed. At twelve minutes past nine the card machines stopped accepting payments. By half past nine the queue outside the bakery had reached the newsagent's door, where someone was trying to buy a newspaper with a phone.

Rafi was halfway through a video call with his sister in another country when her face froze in a peculiar expression. He waited for the little spinning symbol to finish its business. Then he tried again, walked to the window, held his phone above his head, and said a word he disliked using around his daughter.

He had promised his sister they would discuss their mother's birthday. Their mother was living in a care home, and the siblings had been arguing for months about whether she should stay there. Rafi believed the nurses knew her needs better than he did. His sister believed that was precisely why he rarely visited.

The silence gave him a reason not to finish the argument.

Outside, the café owner, Jo, had put a handwritten sign in the window: CASH IF YOU HAVE IT. At the little desk near the door she was making a list of unpaid coffees.

“This is a terrible business model,” Rafi said.

“It was worse before I knew what people owed,” she replied.

A student in a red jacket announced that everyone should simply pay later through an app. Six people explained the problem to him at once. He took off his headphones.

Rafi paid for a coffee with two coins he had found in his coat. Jo wrote his name down anyway.

“Already paid.”

“Yes, but you looked like you needed to be included.”

He sat at the communal table because the other tables were occupied. Beside him was an older man sewing a button onto a coat. At the far end, a courier kept standing up to check whether the traffic lights had changed. The power was still on and the traffic lights worked, but drivers who relied on phone maps had stopped in the wrong lanes to ask directions. Their horns had become a language no one understood.

“I don't normally talk to strangers,” Rafi admitted.

“Neither do I,” said the man with the button. “But apparently the phones have resigned.”

A woman joined them carrying a carton of plant seedlings. She had driven into town to deliver them to a school but could no longer find the person who had ordered them. The school was closed for maintenance. She had no paper address, only an email thread. Jo suggested the public library because the librarian knew everyone, though the library's computers had also lost service.

The woman asked whether she could leave the seedlings somewhere cool. The café was too warm. Rafi happened to be walking home past a covered porch. He offered to carry them part of the way.

This did not make him a generous person, especially not in the grand version of himself he sometimes imagined. He wanted to leave the café because his sister's unfinished sentence was still in his head. Carrying a box provided a reason.

They walked past a hardware shop where a man stood with a broken card reader and a handwritten list of screws. At the corner, two people were arguing over whether a bus would come. The bus did come, but it arrived full and drove on.

The woman with the seedlings was called Mei. She taught part-time and worked in a nursery. She had once lived in Rafi's building, on the other side of the city, before the rent rose.

“Were you there when the lift got stuck?” she asked.

“Which time?”

“That answers it.”

They placed the seedlings on his porch and labelled them with a paper note. Mei went to find the school caretaker. Rafi went inside, put the kettle on, and looked at the frozen face on his phone. He could not call his sister, but he could write down what he wanted to say when he did. He began with an apology. It sounded theatrical. He crossed it out.

By noon he had three pages of notes. They were mostly questions. What did their mother want, and how often could they ask her in a way that she could answer? Who had time to visit? Would moving her closer help or merely change which sibling felt guilty? What did his sister know that he had not asked?

At two the network returned. A storm of messages arrived in the usual order of their timestamps, not in the order of their importance. One was from Mei: THE CARETAKER FOUND ME. KEEP TWO SEEDLINGS IF YOU LIKE. Another was from his sister: WE CAN TALK TOMORROW. I NEED A QUIET EVENING.

He replied before he could stop himself: YOU ONLY CALL WHEN YOU NEED SOMETHING. He saw the words arrive on the screen and immediately hated them. His sister did not answer. He watered the two seedlings, then sat with the phone beside his cup until the tea went cold.

The town's story of the Great Silence soon grew elaborate. People said they had rediscovered community, that they had learnt to look up, that a few hours without a signal had reminded them of what mattered. Jo disliked these summaries.

“I lost a full morning of trade,” she said. “And I have three names I can't read.”

Some customers repaid their debts. A few did not. The man with the button returned to the café with a repaired coat and a tin of needles, which he offered to leave there for anyone who needed one. Jo told him she would rather have the missing payments, and then let him put the tin under the counter.

Rafi rang his sister the next day. The first thing she said was, “That was cruel.”

“I know.”

“You don't know what I ask about. You aren't here.”

They argued. He apologised for the message without asking her to forgive him yet. They were still divided about their mother's care, and his sister did not want to arrange a joint meeting that day. She said she would think about it. He had no way to make her decide faster.

The following week, one of Mei's seedlings produced a first pale leaf on Rafi's porch. He sent the photograph to his sister by mistake. She replied with a picture of a plant growing through a crack in her kitchen tiles.

He typed a reply about the cracks in his kitchen ceiling, deleted it, and sent a photograph of the second seedling instead. This time he checked the recipient before pressing send.


## 4. A Quiet Harvest

The frost came after the blossom, when the orchard had already made its bright promises.

By dawn the apricot trees were silver. Their owner, Nessa, walked between the rows with a torch and felt the frozen grass crack beneath her boots. At the end of each branch the small fruit had turned translucent, as if someone had placed glass beads where there should have been living flesh.

Her father had planted half the trees. She had planted the rest. The trees did not distinguish between them.

The first pickers were due in early summer, more than two months away. Nessa had paid deposits on transport and seasonal accommodation. The labour coordinator on the phone asked whether she would still need the people they had pencilled in.

“I don't know what there will be to pick,” she said.

“Well, let us know soon.”

At midday her neighbour Len drove over carrying a thermos of coffee. He had survived four bad seasons and remembered each as the one that would finish him. He explained how he had burnt hay bales along the windward edge of the property in 1987, how the smoke had drifted the wrong way and how his wife had refused to speak to him for three days.

“You can't heat the sky,” he said.

Nessa drank the coffee and looked at her phone. A message had come from Asha, who had picked fruit in the district for the last two summers. She was deciding between the planned summer harvest and a nursery job farther north; she needed to know whether the orchard work might disappear before she declined the other offer. The childcare she had provisionally organised would not follow her from one district to another.

Nessa wrote: THERE SHOULD BE SOMETHING. She looked at the sentence until it seemed dishonest. She deleted it and rang instead.

“I think we've lost most of the crop,” she said. “I can't promise the hours we planned.”

Asha was quiet for a moment.

“Do you know when you'll know?”

“No.”

“Then I'd rather know that.”

Nessa had hoped the truth would feel like relief. It did not.

Over the following fortnight the frost revealed its strange boundaries. One row beside the shelterbelt remained green. A stand of trees near the road had lost everything. Several branches appeared healthy until the fruit split in the sun. Nessa measured, counted and made plans that shifted as she worked.

Some neighbours encouraged her to ask the workers to come anyway. There was pruning, clearing and fence maintenance. Others advised cancelling all the contracts before she lost more money. Nessa had enough money for a limited amount of paid spring maintenance, not for every promised harvest shift if the crop failed. She had another loan payment due. The figures did not bend because she loved the orchard.

Asha arrived one afternoon with her daughter, Lila, who wore yellow gumboots and collected stones shaped like eggs. She had come to see whether any work was certain.

Nessa spread the numbers on the kitchen table. She explained which work she could pay for, which was uncertain, and when the next decision would be made. Nessa could not offer firm harvest hours and did not pretend that Asha should wait for her decision. She offered to put her current estimate and uncertainty in writing, including the date she would update it, so Asha could decide whether to pursue the other job. Any existing agreements and entitlements, she said, would have to be checked separately rather than settled across her kitchen table.

Asha scanned the paper slowly. “You should put a date on it,” she said. “And your name where the agency can ring you.”

Nessa borrowed a pen.

Outside, Lila had found a single apricot in a sheltered corner. It was hard and green. She brought it inside as though it were a prize.

“Can we eat this one?”

“Not yet,” Nessa said. “It might not grow properly.”

Lila looked wounded by the uncertainty of fruit.

Asha took the nursery job north the next week. Nessa delayed decisions about the reduced harvest until the fruit set was clearer; in the meantime she repaired irrigation pipes with the workers she could pay. Len brought his tractor to lift a broken post; Nessa paid him in fuel, not promises. They did not call it sharing luck. They were trying to keep useful things from deteriorating further.

The district newspaper asked for a photograph of the ruined fruit. Nessa declined. Then she wondered whether publicity might bring help with the insurance dispute. She rang back and agreed to a photograph of the orchard, with no workers staged beside the damaged trees. The story ran under the headline: LOCAL GROWER DETERMINED TO BOUNCE BACK.

She cut it out, not because she liked the headline but because Lila had asked to see herself in the photograph. Lila was nowhere in it.

In midsummer, when the small surviving crop was finally ready, Asha returned for two paid days to help sort and pack fruit before starting a course elsewhere. The sorting shed smelt of cardboard and warm apricots. The damaged rows beyond the open door still carried gaps where boxes should have been.

“Did you make enough to keep the place?” Asha asked.

“I think so. Not enough to make it easy.”

“Good,” Asha said, then stopped. “I mean good that you know. Not good that it happened.”

Nessa laughed, the first proper laugh she had had in weeks.

On the last evening, Nessa packed a small box of apricots from the surviving trees for Asha's train ride. Asha took three and left the rest.

“I can't carry all that,” she said. “You can give them to someone else.”

Nessa stood beside the gate after the car had gone. One of the three apricots had rolled out of Asha's bag and lay by the gravel, its skin bruised where it had struck the latch.

She picked it up. The fruit was ripe enough to come apart under her thumb. In the distance a tractor turned at the end of an almost empty row.

## 5. The Map Without a Bridge

From the lookout above the valley, the river looked like a strip of old silver.

In the council office, on the wall beside the photocopier, it was a blue line two millimetres wide. Peni had been drawing that line for three months. It was part of a new walking map intended to bring visitors into the district: river paths, picnic areas, old railway cuttings and a route across the historic footbridge.

The bridge was the whole reason the loop worked. Without it walkers would have to turn around, and a twenty-minute walk would become nearly two hours on uneven ground.

The bridge had been closed for sixteen months.

Peni discovered this from an email sent by a volunteer working group after the map had gone to print. The group wanted to know why the bridge remained on the recommended route. The little closure symbol, which Peni remembered placing carefully over the blue line, had vanished from the final export. One version of the design software had flattened the annotation into an invisible layer.

Two thousand maps sat in cartons by the visitor centre. Another three thousand were due to arrive on Monday.

Peni did what people sometimes do when a mistake is large and their own name is near it. He searched for an explanation that would make the mistake less his. The closure had been poorly communicated. The manager had approved the proof. The printer had not checked the file. All of these things were true.

He rang the visitor centre.

“The footbridge on the loop is still closed,” he said.

“I know,” said Frances, who had answered. “People have been coming back angry for months. One couple followed the old directions to the locked gate and missed their last bus back. We found them a taxi, but they paid for it.”

“From the old maps?”

“From hearing about the loop at the motel. The new ones are supposed to fix it.”

He explained the problem. She was quiet and then asked a practical question.

“How many are already out?”

“None, as far as I know.”

“That helps.”

By lunchtime a meeting had gathered in the council office. The tourism manager wanted to withdraw the maps and reprint them. The finance manager wanted stickers over the bridge symbol. Someone suggested including a note that users should check conditions before setting off. Peni thought the note could sound as though the missing bridge were the walker's fault.

Frances arrived from the visitor centre carrying a marked-up copy of the map. She placed it on the table with the familiar firmness of somebody who had spent too many mornings apologising for decisions made elsewhere.

“The western side of the river isn't a loop,” she said. “But it is a good walk. People like it. You could show the bridge as closed and print a clear out-and-back route.”

The tourism manager objected that the map had been sold to local businesses as an exciting new loop.

“Then tell them what changed,” Frances replied.

Peni had not considered that he could create a second route on the reverse side. He sat down at the computer and drew it while the others spoke. A shorter walk would begin near the old station and end at a viewing platform. The path was already maintained. There were no steep steps, but the platform was narrow and not suitable for every mobility aid. Frances insisted that the map state those limits plainly.

The sticker solution was approved for the first two thousand copies, with a separate route notice at the counter. The remaining print run was paused. That afternoon, Peni stood in the visitor centre with three other people, checking each sticker by hand. One stuck to his sleeve. Another curled at the edge of a damp map. He began putting aside the copies he did not trust.

A man with a canvas satchel watched them from the doorway.

“What's wrong with the bridge?” he asked.

“It's unsafe,” Peni said. “And we failed to mark it closed.”

“Why not just repair it?”

Peni did not know the answer. The bridge belonged to a different budget and had been argued over for years. The man frowned at the map, looked at the new route, and took one.

“Good that you said,” he replied.

The revised maps did not solve the problems they had been designed around. The café beside the far end of the bridge still lost passing trade. A motel owner complained that the council had exaggerated what visitors could do. A group of wheelchair users said the route descriptions needed more than a line about steps. Frances started a small log of those complaints so that corrections could be made in the next edition.

Three months later the finance manager asked Peni whether he considered the project a success.

Peni thought of the money spent, the couple who had paid for a taxi, and the family who had thanked Frances for warning them about the platform.

“It was a correction,” he said. “Ask me about success when the bridge has a safe alternative.”

The bridge remained closed through the next summer. A child drew a bright red cross over it on one of the printed maps and pinned the page to the visitor centre board beside a photograph of the river. The child had added a little arrow marked VIEW FROM HERE.

Frances left the drawing on display. Visitors began asking about the viewpoint rather than the bridge, and several discovered the shorter route. It was not the route Peni had wanted to offer. He still walked it occasionally, checking where the gravel washed away after rain.

One afternoon he reached the viewing platform and found a couple sitting side by side, looking upstream. They were not looking for a crossing. They had brought a flask and a bird book and seemed in no hurry to go anywhere else.

He was pleased they had found the path. He also knew that a person standing at the closed bridge on the far bank might feel quite differently.

Both would be on the next map.

## 6. The Unmeasured Salt

There were three versions of the soup, and neither sister wanted to admit she had invented one.

The first was written on a square of cardboard that had once been part of a cereal box: **ONIONS. LENTILS. LEMON. SALT UNTIL IT TASTES RIGHT.** The second lived in Salma's memory and contained tomatoes, though her younger sister Leena said their mother had never used tomatoes. The third was simmering in a dented blue pot on the stove, where it had already turned an alarming shade of orange.

Their mother had been dead for six weeks. The landlord wanted the flat emptied by Monday, and neither sister had agreed what to do with the pot.

Salma lived three train stops away. For the last two years she had come most evenings to bring groceries, chase prescriptions and sit at the small kitchen table while their mother watched television at a volume that irritated the neighbours. Leena lived overseas. She had flown in for the funeral and was trying to make five years of missed Tuesdays fit into nine days of leave.

“It's tomato,” Leena said, dipping a spoon. “You can taste it.”

“That would be because I put some in.”

“Why would you?”

“Because that's how she made it at the end.”

Leena set the spoon down with a care that was worse than slamming it.

Their mother had called the dish *rain soup*. She made it on cold evenings, or when someone had failed an exam, or when there was no money for anything besides lentils and an onion. As children, they had eaten it with bread torn straight from the loaf. Their father ate it with chilli and complained when there was none. He had been gone so long that he survived in the story only as a man who wanted more chilli.

On the windowsill stood a row of jars without lids. A spider had made its web between two of them. Leena watched it for a moment and said, “I want the pot.”

Salma was chopping parsley. The knife struck the board a little harder.

“You've got a kitchen full of new things.”

“It's not about what I need.”

“No. You weren't here for what she needed.”

Both women stopped. The extractor fan made a clicking noise. From the courtyard below came the sharp squeak of a scooter wheel.

Leena reached for her coat. She had practised answering that accusation on the plane, in the taxi and while lying awake on the sofa. None of the rehearsed sentences survived hearing it aloud.

“You could have asked me to come back earlier,” she said.

“I did.”

“You asked when I had a job interview.”

“I know when I asked.”

Leena pushed her chair under the table. It caught on a loose tile. She sat again, angry at the chair for making departure difficult.

The soup boiled over. Salma grabbed a cloth, swore when the handle burnt her fingers and tipped the pot back from the heat. A streak of orange ran down the front of the cooker. Neither sister moved to wipe it away.

“She started adding tomatoes because she couldn't taste the lemon,” Salma said finally. “The medicine changed things. She wanted stronger flavours.”

“She hated tomatoes.”

“She hated lots of things near the end.”

Leena looked at the cardboard recipe. Their mother's handwriting was broad and slanted. The word *right* had been underlined twice.

“I thought if we made it the old way,” Leena said, “I'd remember being here.”

Salma took two bowls from the cupboard. One had a chip shaped like a crescent moon; their mother had insisted it was the good one because it held more soup.

“Have it,” Salma said, meaning the pot.

“That isn't how I wanted to get it.”

“I know. But if you leave it behind because we've argued, I'll hate you for that too.”

They laughed once, unwillingly. It was the exact sound their mother used to make when a difficult conversation became ridiculous and stayed difficult.

The soup tasted of tomato and too much salt. Salma ate half a bowl. Leena added lemon, tasted it again and said it was worse. They finished it with bread from the corner shop, because neither wanted to cook another thing.

Afterwards Leena washed the pot in hot water and set it on a tea towel to dry. She found a repair on one handle: a thin loop of wire, twisted so tightly that the metal pressed into the enamel. She had never noticed it before.

“Did she do that?” she asked.

“Probably. Or the man downstairs. I don't know.”

Leena ran a thumb over the wire. “I thought I knew everything about this thing.”

Their phone rang. It was the landlord confirming the time for Monday's inspection. Salma answered. When she hung up, she said the landlord would charge them if they left the cooker dirty.

“We'd better clean it,” Leena said.

They worked without speaking for a while. Salma held the oven door open while Leena reached in with a cloth. The burnt orange came off slowly. Some of the old stains beneath it did not.

At the end Leena wrapped the blue pot in yesterday's newspaper, then unwrapped it and put it back on the shelf. Her flight was on Sunday. There was room in her suitcase if she left behind a pair of shoes.

“Take it tomorrow,” Salma said.

Leena nodded. She folded the newspaper into a square and put it beside the pot.

A late bus hissed at the stop below. The flat had grown so quiet that they could hear the driver tell someone to move down the aisle. Salma made tea. When she turned around, Leena was scraping the last spoonful of orange soup into a container.

“What are you doing?”

“Taking it with me. I want to see whether it's worse cold.”

“You always liked it cold.”

“I did not.”

Salma opened her mouth to argue, then chose the chipped bowl from the drying rack. She set it beside Leena without a word. The spoon rested across its rim, balanced for a moment before it slid into the bowl with a small, familiar clink.


## 7. The Night of No Stars

The astronomy evening was advertised for six weeks, and for six weeks the sky was clear.

On the seventh Friday, when the town's first public telescope was finally set up outside the library, clouds arrived from the west in a thick grey procession. A small crowd gathered under the eaves to watch them. The telescope stood in the square like a machine that had forgotten what it was for.

Zoya had arranged the evening because she remembered being twelve and looking through a borrowed pair of binoculars at the moon. She had been astonished that something so distant could have sharp edges and shadows. Ever since, she had wanted other people to have that first look. Now she worked at the library and had persuaded a local camera club to lend a telescope. The equipment had come in three large cases with forty-two small parts and a manual written for someone who already knew what they were doing.

She had rehearsed setting it up three times. On the fourth, in front of an audience, one of the tripod legs refused to lock.

A boy in a striped woollen hat asked when they would see Saturn. Zoya said that she would try, if the sky cleared. A man with a camera said it looked like rain. His companion pointed out that the man had said the same thing at every outdoor event since Easter.

An older woman, Alma, arrived using a walking stick and sat on the low wall beside the library. She had walked slowly from the retirement flats to be there. When Zoya asked whether she wanted a chair, Alma nodded but said she had chosen the wall because it faced the open sky.

"I haven't looked through a telescope since school," she said.

"What did you see?"

"Nothing. The teacher spent the whole lesson trying to focus it."

Zoya laughed. Alma looked pleased by the fact that someone had finally understood the joke.

At half past seven the wind grew stronger. The camera club volunteer offered to pack the telescope away before it was damaged. Zoya felt a sharp disappointment and then an unreasonable anger at the weather. The library had printed posters, the club had lent equipment, and a dozen people had rearranged their evenings. She had done everything she could think of except arrange a clear sky.

A child asked whether the telescope could be pointed at the streetlight.

"It could," the volunteer said. "But it wouldn't tell you much about the sky."

The child asked why not. The volunteer began an explanation about distance and brightness, then stopped and tried again with a simpler example. Another child wanted to know why the moon sometimes appeared during the day. Somebody asked whether the stars were really different colours.

Zoya realised that they were already having an astronomy evening, though not the one she had planned.

She brought out a tray of paper and pencils. The volunteer drew a rough diagram of a telescope. Alma told a story about learning the constellations by the names her grandfather used, most of which were unfamiliar to the children. A teenager showed the group how an astronomy app predicted the sky behind the clouds, then admitted that the app could not make a cloud disappear. The man with the camera took a photograph of the empty-looking sky. In the picture the clouds appeared surprisingly beautiful.

At eight o'clock a gap opened near the horizon. The volunteer turned the telescope carefully towards a bright point of light. Several people queued. Just as the boy in the striped hat reached the front, the clouds closed again.

He looked through the eyepiece and saw only grey.

"That was my chance," he said.

Zoya wanted to tell him there would be other nights, but she did not know whether his family would bring him back, or whether the library could arrange the telescope again. It seemed unfair to promise what she could not guarantee.

"It was a chance," she said. "And it didn't work."

The volunteer offered to point the telescope at the streetlight, where the water in the air made an odd bright halo. Two other children queued to see it. The boy shook his head.

“I came for Saturn.”

“I know,” Zoya said.

He went inside with his grandfather and kept his hat on. Zoya could hear him asking whether the clouds had been there yesterday. His grandfather did not try to turn the answer into good news.

When the event ended, people helped carry the chairs inside. Alma stayed to finish a cup of tea. She asked whether Zoya was disappointed.

"A bit," Zoya admitted. "I wanted everyone to see something extraordinary."

"You were asking a lot of a Friday," Alma replied.

They washed the cups together. Zoya wrote down the questions she had not been able to answer, including one about whether a telescope could make a star look like a disc and another about how astronomers knew the age of light. She planned to ask the camera club whether they would help with a second evening. The weather would decide whether there was anything to see.

A week later a drawing arrived at the library. It showed the telescope pointed at a sky full of clouds. On the back the boy had written: **WE DIDN'T SEE SATURN.** Underneath, in smaller letters: **I STILL WANT TO.**

Zoya pinned it beside the event calendar. She asked the club whether the telescope could return. No one answered that afternoon. She left the space beside next month's Friday blank.

## 8. The Five O'Clock Ferry

There were twenty-seven passengers waiting when the ferry captain decided not to leave.

The harbour was sheltered, the sky was pale and the water near the terminal looked ordinary. Beyond the headland, however, a strong crosswind had developed. The weather station showed gusts that made the narrow channel difficult for a vessel of that size. Mae had crossed the route for nineteen years. She knew what the ferry could do on a fair day and what a sudden change could ask of it.

She announced a delay of one hour.

A man carrying a cake box demanded to know who would pay for the ruined party. A woman in a suit said she had a flight to catch. A fisherman said the wind would drop as soon as the tide changed. Mae listened to each and said that she would reassess the crossing at six.

The terminal office had a kettle and three chairs. Mae sat there with her first mate, Eli, and reviewed the weather. Eli had been training for two years and was keen to prove he could handle a rough crossing.

“We've done worse,” he said.

“Worse than what?”

“Worse than this.”

Mae turned the screen so he could see the latest gusts at the exposed turn. His confidence slipped.

“Not there,” he admitted.

At six the forecast had worsened. Mae cancelled the sailing.

Passengers gathered at the counter. A woman asked whether there was accommodation on this side of the water; the last bus had gone. The terminal manager began making calls. The man with the cake box stared at the floor. He had been bringing a birthday cake to his father, whose health was failing. He had told his family he would be there by dinner.

Mae knew that a cancellation could cause real harm. On her first year as captain she had missed a crossing due to a maintenance fault, and a passenger had spent the night separated from a child in hospital. The memory had never left her. Safety decisions were not clean choices between inconvenience and disaster. They were judgments under uncertainty, each carrying different costs.

The fisherman approached her away from the crowd.

“You're overcautious,” he said. “The wind won't last.”

“Perhaps.”

“I've been on that water longer than you've been alive.”

“Perhaps,” she said again, though this time she did not believe it.

He smiled despite himself.

“What happens if you're wrong?”

“Then people have had a miserable evening when they might have crossed safely.”

“And if I'm wrong?”

Mae looked through the terminal window at the harbour lights.

“Then we may have a different evening entirely.”

The wind dropped at seven. It rose again at eight. In the middle of the night a larger vessel reported trouble in the channel, though it reached port without serious injury. The ferry company later described Mae's cancellation as prudent. She was wary of the compliment. She had made the decision before that report existed. A different night might have produced no incident at all.

The man with the cake box slept in a motel room the company helped arrange. The terminal manager found a refrigerator for the cake. It was a plain sponge with chocolate icing and a message in careful white letters: **EIGHTY TODAY.**

At breakfast the man told Mae he had spoken to his father by phone. The old man had laughed at the story and said he would enjoy his birthday in instalments.

“He could have been upset,” the man said. “He might have had reason.”

“Yes,” Mae replied.

“I'm still upset.”

“You can be.”

When they crossed the next morning, the channel was calm. Eli took the helm under supervision. Mae watched the water beyond the headland and thought about how little the smooth passage proved about yesterday.

At the far terminal the man with the cake box disembarked with the other passengers. He stopped at the ramp and held the cake high.

“Made it,” he said.

Mae raised a hand. She did not know whether the father would be awake when his son arrived, or whether the sponge would taste any good after a night in a refrigerator. She knew only that the ferry had arrived and that everyone aboard could step safely onto the shore.

Later that afternoon, the company held a review of the cancellation. The manager wanted a short statement for the website. Eli wanted the report to mention that the captain had resisted pressure from passengers. Mae wanted it to explain the weather data, the service limitations and what the company could do better for stranded people.

“Don't make me the whole story,” she said.

The manager looked at her, surprised. The cancellation had been her decision.

“Yes,” Mae said. “That's why I need to explain it.”


# Part III — What Remains Between Us

*The effects of a life are rarely contained within the life itself.*

## 9. The Names Left Off

The museum had kept a register of its volunteers for sixty-one years.

It was a large green book with a cracked spine, stored in a cupboard beside the old fire extinguisher. Each year a new page had been added. Some entries included occupations, dates and titles. Others consisted of a first name written hurriedly in pencil. The people who had donated money were usually named in ink.

When the museum prepared for its anniversary, the committee decided to make a wall of photographs and names. Visitors could see the people who had kept the place alive.

Ellen, the museum's part-time archivist, liked the idea until she opened the book.

The earliest pages named every committee chair but rarely the people who cleaned the exhibition cases, copied labels or made tea during school visits. One photograph showed a group of six women in matching aprons beside a table of donated cakes. The caption identified a man standing behind them as **Museum President, 1971**. The women had no names at all.

Ellen brought the photograph to the anniversary meeting. The committee chair, Gavin, examined it and said they should do their best to identify the women.

“We can't put names in if we don't know them,” he added.

“Of course.”

“And we shouldn't make the exhibition about what's missing.”

Ellen looked at the picture. The women were smiling towards the camera, while the man behind them appeared to be looking at something else.

“What if what's missing is part of the history?” she asked.

Gavin sighed. He had given thirty years to the museum and disliked being made to feel that those years amounted to an error.

“People worked hard,” he said. “We can't rewrite that.”

“I don't want to.”

The following week Ellen invited local residents to help identify faces, with a notice that any suggested names would need checking before display. She set up a table at the library and brought copies of photographs. By lunchtime seven people had visited. One man identified his aunt in the 1971 picture. Another remembered that the woman on the left had worked at the old telephone exchange but could not recall her surname. A retired teacher recognised a scarf but not a face.

Then a woman named Nola arrived with a small folder of letters. Her mother had volunteered at the museum for two decades. Nola had kept the letters because her mother had been careful with her words and the paper still smelt faintly of lavender.

They read them together. In one letter the mother wrote that she loved the museum because it gave her something to do after her husband died. In another she complained that the committee had bought a new display cabinet but would not replace the leaking kettle. The letters were lively, sometimes funny, and occasionally severe.

“Can I use these?” Ellen asked.

Nola touched the folder.

“Some of them. Not the one about her sister. That's family business.”

They sat with the pages and chose three short passages. Ellen made copies and left the originals with Nola.

As the exhibition developed, the blank spaces became harder to conceal. For the photographs that had names, Ellen wrote them clearly. Where the records were incomplete, she printed **Not yet identified** and an invitation for verified corrections. Beside the 1971 photograph she added a small note about the limits of the surviving register: it preserved some kinds of contribution more faithfully than others.

Gavin objected to the note. “It makes us look careless.”

“We were careless,” said Ellen, then immediately regretted the sharpness.

“Some of those people aren't here to answer.”

“That's why we need to be precise.”

They argued for half an hour and left the meeting without agreement. At home Ellen reread the museum's files and discovered that Gavin had spent many evenings repairing broken cabinets without claiming money or credit. The anniversary records mentioned those repairs only once, under an expense heading marked MATERIALS.

She had been too ready to make him a symbol of the problem.

She wrote to him, acknowledging the repairs and asking whether he would check the dates. He replied with the missing dates, but not an apology. He did not want his name added to the display. He also refused to approve the note about gaps in the register.

At the next committee meeting they argued again. Ellen wanted the note to stay; Gavin thought visitors would read it as a verdict on volunteers who could no longer defend themselves. Another committee member asked them to make the disagreement visible only through what could actually be supported. The note stayed, with a clearer description of the surviving records and their limits. Gavin voted against it.

At the anniversary opening, people studied the photographs more closely than Ellen had expected. They leaned towards the glass, pointed at faces and disputed dates. A child asked why there were so many blank labels. Nola told him that some people were never asked to sign their names. Gavin, standing beside the doorway, said that many people hadn't wanted to sign either. The child looked from one adult to the other and went to find his grandmother.

Later that evening Gavin stood beside the 1971 photograph. He had brought a pen and a small piece of paper. He wrote down the name of a woman his mother had known, including the year she moved away, but asked Ellen not to display it until someone could confirm it.

“She made the cakes,” he said. “The whole town knew that.”

“Perhaps they did then.”

Ellen added the note to the research folder. The exhibit would remain open for another month, and she knew that several names might never be recovered. A record could be improved without becoming complete.

At closing time Ellen switched off the lights one by one. Gavin had left his jacket hanging on the chair where he usually sat. She folded it and placed it in the cupboard for him to collect.

In the final room the photograph glowed faintly under the emergency light: six women in aprons and one man behind them. Next to one woman, the small label still read **Not yet identified**. Ellen could hear Gavin outside moving the folding chairs without asking for help.

## 10. The Path Through the Reeds

For years there had been a narrow path across the wetland, made by people who wanted to reach the beach without walking around the road.

It began behind the sports ground, passed a drainage ditch, and disappeared among reeds tall enough to hide a grown person. At the far end, children emerged onto a strip of sand where tiny crabs vanished into holes when they approached.

Aroha had walked the path since she was nine. In summer she went barefoot. In winter she wore her father's old gumboots, which filled with water because one seam had split.

When she was seventeen, the council proposed to widen the path and lay gravel so that more people could use it. The design included a small boardwalk over the softest section. A local business offered to pay for a sign at the entrance. The beach would become part of a walking circuit between the town centre and the waterfront.

Aroha was pleased. Her younger brother used a wheelchair and had never reached that part of the beach. Their family had stopped carrying him down rough tracks when he grew too heavy. A boardwalk might change what was possible for him.

Then the conservation group raised an objection.

The reeds were used by birds that nested low near the water. In the breeding season a wider path would bring people and dogs closer to the nest sites. The proposal included a fence, but its line cut through a patch of marsh where the group had counted several nests.

At the public meeting, Aroha sat between her brother, Wiremu, and an older volunteer called Mrs Hall. The councillor explained the design with a slide showing a graceful curve of timber across green vegetation. In the picture there were no birds, no mud, and no children struggling with an uneven path.

A conservation worker showed photographs of the nesting area. Several people said the birds should come first. Others said it was unfair to protect a view that only people with strong legs could enjoy. Someone suggested that dogs could simply be banned. A man at the back asked whether the boardwalk was needed at all.

Wiremu raised his hand.

“I'd like to go there,” he said. “But not if it means the birds have to move for me.”

Aroha knew he was trying to be generous. She also knew he was tired of being told that access was a problem to be solved only when everyone else had been satisfied. She wished he had not felt the need to apologise for wanting to see the sea.

After the meeting, Mrs Hall asked whether they would visit the wetland with her and the conservation worker. They went on a cold morning. Wiremu waited on the solid path beside the sports ground while Aroha and the others crossed the muddy section. The worker pointed out tiny tracks in the mud and showed them where the nests were usually found. Aroha had passed the reeds for years without knowing what lived between them.

She returned to Wiremu and described what she had seen. He listened and then asked whether the first section of the path could be made accessible without going through the nesting area. There was a raised bank on the eastern side, used by maintenance vehicles, which reached a lookout above the beach. It was longer, but firmer.

The worker looked surprised. “Maybe. We'd need to check the slope and the landowner's agreement.”

Mrs Hall said the raised bank had been there since the old drainage works. It had not appeared on the planning map because it was not an official public path.

The council commissioned a new assessment. It found that the bank could be used for an accessible route, though not without work and cost. The proposed boardwalk through the reeds was set aside. The new design included a viewing platform above the beach, a shorter unpaved path for people who preferred it, and seasonal signs explaining why the nesting area should remain undisturbed.

Not everyone was happy. A coastal walking group complained that the revised path was too long. A family living beside the bank worried that the new route would put strangers directly outside their bedroom windows; the original drawing showed no screening or boundary between the ramp and their fence. The conservation group wanted more fencing. The council had to negotiate access rights and find additional money. Construction was delayed for a year, and the final plan added screening near the house and a lower path surface that would need regular maintenance.

Aroha went away to study during that time. When she returned in summer, the platform had finally opened. She took Wiremu there on an afternoon when the light was low and the wind carried the smell of salt. The ramp was wider than she expected. They reached the top together.

Below them the wetland moved quietly in the breeze. A small dark bird stepped between the reeds and vanished. Wiremu took a photograph, then another, and complained that the bird refused to pose.

“It's not here for the camera,” Aroha said.

“I know,” he replied. “But it could show some manners.”

They stayed until the sun slipped behind the hills. On their way back they passed the old path, now marked with a request to avoid it during nesting season. The sign explained why, and gave directions to the longer route. Aroha saw footprints in the mud beyond it. Some people would continue to take the shortcut.

The wetland had not been saved once and for all, nor had access become perfect. The platform did not bring Wiremu onto the sand itself. Yet he had stood above the beach without being carried, and the reeds below had been given more room.

On the bus home he looked through his photographs and found one in which the bird was almost visible: a blur of dark feathers against pale stems.

“That's the one,” he said.

Aroha could not see it at first. Then he pointed, and she did.

## 11. The Rules of the Room

The community theatre was offered a gift of enough money to replace its failing heating system.

The donor, Mrs Benton, had attended performances for forty years. She wore bright scarves and applauded with both hands above her head. Her husband had acted in the theatre's first production and their daughter had learnt to paint scenery there. The building mattered to her.

The offer came in a letter to the committee, along with two conditions. The new heating system was to carry a plaque bearing her family's name, and her grandson was to be given a speaking part in the next play.

The committee met on a Tuesday evening under an electric heater that clicked like a dissatisfied insect. Seven people sat around a folding table. The director, Sal, read the conditions aloud twice.

“The plaque is fine,” said one member.

“The speaking part isn't,” said another.

“He's talented,” someone pointed out.

“How do you know?”

“He was in the school musical.”

Sal closed the letter. She was tired of rehearsing in a cold building. The heating had failed twice that winter and they had cancelled a workshop for older performers because the hall could not be warmed safely. Accepting the gift would change things. It might also make every future casting decision harder to trust.

The treasurer suggested taking the money and offering the grandson a small part.

“Then what do we say to everyone who auditions?” Sal asked.

“We say the theatre is open because of his grandmother.”

“That's not an answer.”

They voted to ask Mrs Benton whether the conditions were negotiable.

Sal went to see her the following afternoon. The house was filled with theatre programmes, framed photographs and a biscuit tin of old tickets. Mrs Benton poured tea and explained that her grandson had been shy since changing schools. A part in the play, she believed, would give him confidence.

“He needs to know he belongs somewhere,” she said.

Sal had expected vanity and found worry instead. This made the conversation harder, not easier.

“He can audition,” Sal said. “We can make that process welcoming and give feedback. But I can't promise him a part.”

Mrs Benton frowned. “You're being very formal about a children's production.”

“That's when it matters to the children.”

Mrs Benton looked past Sal at a photograph of her husband in costume. “He got his first part because the director was his friend.”

“Perhaps that's a reason to do things differently now.”

It was not a clever thing to say. Mrs Benton put down her cup. For several minutes they discussed the heating instead.

A week later a revised offer arrived. The donor would pay for the system with the family name on a small plaque in the foyer, but only if the theatre agreed to give her grandson a guaranteed place in a new youth workshop. Sal asked the workshop facilitator what she thought.

“We don't currently guarantee places to anyone,” she said. “We have a waiting list.”

Sal took the question back to the committee. They could create an extra workshop if there was enough funding for a facilitator and accessible transport. But guaranteeing one place to one child would still require turning away another if the room were full. The committee drafted a proposal: the donor's gift could support the heating, and a separate programme fund could expand the youth workshop for all applicants under published criteria. Her grandson would be welcome to apply, but not entitled to preference.

Mrs Benton was offended. For a month the gift seemed lost.

The theatre rehearsed in coats. One Saturday, the cast painted scenery with numb fingers while a kettle warmed water for brush cleaning. Sal wondered whether a principled refusal had made other people's lives unnecessarily difficult. She worried about the older performers who had stayed home and the volunteers who were paying extra for electricity.

The treasurer brought a grant application to the next meeting. It was worth trying, he said, though the deadline was months away and the fund was oversubscribed. The youth workshop still had a waiting list. The heating supplier would not hold its quotation indefinitely.

Sal voted to apply. She also voted to cancel the winter production rather than ask performers to rehearse in a room that could not be heated safely. That was the moment the decision became unpopular.

The actor playing the lead had taken unpaid leave for rehearsals. Two volunteers had booked travel for relatives who wanted to see the show. The costume maker had bought fabric she could not return. None of them had offered to serve as the cost of principle. At the following meeting, one member stood up and asked why the committee had not put Mrs Benton's offer to the whole theatre earlier.

“We did,” Sal said.

“You put the refusal to us. Not the choice.”

Sal could have said the conditions were plainly unfair. They were. She also knew the member was right about the timing. They had debated the gift in a small room and told everyone else what conscience required after the consequences were unavoidable.

“I should have asked sooner,” she said.

The grant was unsuccessful. A letter arrived in spring praising the theatre's community contribution and explaining that the money had gone to buildings in greater need. The treasurer read the letter aloud. Nobody thanked him for it.

Mrs Benton came to the theatre a week later to collect a borrowed photograph of her husband. She found Sal sitting on the edge of the empty stage, where the rehearsal tape still marked positions on the boards.

“Did you get the grant?” Mrs Benton asked.

“No.”

“Then I suppose you want to talk again.”

“Not if it means buying a role.”

Mrs Benton took the photograph from its frame. “You think I'm trying to buy your theatre.”

“I think you're trying to help your grandson. I also think those are different things.”

“He was afraid of auditions. He still is.”

Sal looked at the painted tape. She could imagine the boy standing under lights, looking for a familiar face in the dark. She could also imagine a child who had earned a part being told it belonged to someone else.

“The workshop has a waiting list,” she said. “I can send you information about other places he might enjoy, but I won't put him ahead of the children already waiting.”

Mrs Benton folded the photograph into a paper bag.

“You'll be cold again next winter,” she said, and went home.

The theatre moved rehearsals into a borrowed classroom for a while. The room had good heating and terrible acoustics; it also closed at nine, just when the performers were beginning to get somewhere. Two people stopped coming because the evening bus no longer reached them. Sal arranged a lift for one, but not for the other. He told her not to make a special case of him. She did not know whether that was pride, weariness or an instruction she ought to obey.

By midsummer they had raised enough to repair one part of the heating system, not replace it. The treasurer kept the estimate for the full job in a folder beside the accounts. The next winter schedule remained shorter than before.

On an evening when rain struck the classroom windows, the company rehearsed a scene about a family trying to share a table that was too small. The actors argued over who should stand. Someone laughed at the wrong moment. Sal thought of Mrs Benton's husband, whose photograph was no longer in the foyer, and of the boy she had never met.

At the end of rehearsal she peeled a strip of tape from the classroom floor. Under it the wood was a paler colour. She rolled the tape into a tight ball and put it in her pocket; they would need the room clean by morning.

Outside, the last bus pulled away as the cast reached the gate. One of the younger performers began running after it, then stopped. The road was already empty.


## 12. What the Tide Keeps

The last person to maintain the old tide gauge was a woman named June.

For forty years she had walked down to the harbour wall before sunrise, opened the small steel box and written the height of the water in a notebook. The gauge itself was simple: painted marks on a vertical board, a weathered ladder, and a mechanism that recorded the changes between visits. The figures were sent to an office on the mainland. Sometimes they mattered to shipping, sometimes to flood warnings, and sometimes to people who wanted to know how a season compared with the years before.

June liked the early hour. She liked the sound of rope against a bollard and the way the town was still asleep when she passed its windows.

When she was seventy-eight the office replaced the manual readings with a remote sensor. A technician arrived, fitted new equipment and showed her a screen where the tide appeared as a moving line.

“You can see it from anywhere,” he said.

June looked at the line. “Can you smell it?”

He laughed, though he was not sure whether she was joking.

The office thanked her for her service. A formal letter arrived with a photograph of the new sensor. June folded it into the back of her notebook and kept going down to the harbour most mornings anyway.

There she met a boy named Ben, who had begun fishing before school because his house was crowded and the wall was quiet. He watched her count the marks on the board and asked what she was doing.

“Keeping a record,” she said.

“Of what?”

“The same thing in different ways.”

He thought about this. “Does the water ever do exactly the same thing twice?”

“Near enough for some purposes.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“No,” June said. “It isn't.”

She began showing him the old notebooks. He was thirteen and more interested in the storms than the ordinary days. He liked the pages where June had drawn little arrows, noting wind direction and floating debris. She showed him that a high tide was not automatically a dangerous tide, and that a calm morning did not erase what had happened the day before.

When Ben's family moved away, he took a photograph of the gauge and left June a note: **I'll check the water where we go.** He never sent an address. June was disappointed, then annoyed at herself for being disappointed. A boy could leave a town without being required to carry its old people in his pockets.

The next winter June fell and broke her wrist. She spent three weeks at home with her arm in a sling, listening to weather forecasts that made little sense without the harbour in front of her. Her daughter visited and suggested that June should stop going down to the wall alone.

June argued. Then, on a wet morning, she opened a notebook and found she could not hold the pen properly. She had to accept that some things were no longer easy.

She asked the library whether it would keep the old records. The librarian was pleased but said the notebooks needed an access agreement before they could be made public. Some pages contained names, private complaints and observations about people who had not expected to become part of an archive.

June spent several afternoons reading them with the librarian. On a page about a storm she found a note about a neighbour's drinking; on another, a family argument overheard on the wharf. She wanted to strike both out. The librarian asked her not to mark the originals, which had other people's histories tangled through the tide readings.

“If I give you these,” June asked, “can anyone walk in and read what I said about people?”

“Not without an agreement,” the librarian said. “We can keep the originals safe, restrict the sensitive pages for now and let people use copies with private passages obscured. The copies would say where something was withheld. We'd need to review the arrangement as time passes.”

June looked down at her handwriting. She had meant it to help her remember the weather, not to give strangers opinions about her neighbours. She asked the librarian to write the proposed terms down so she could think about them before signing.

On a page from 1996 June found a drawing of a gull standing on a bucket, made while she waited for the tide to turn. She was surprised at how accurately she had caught its bad temper.

The agreed notebooks went to the library in two boxes. June kept the earliest one, the book she had begun when her husband was still alive. She could not explain why that one belonged at home, only that she wanted it there.

In spring the harbour wall was rebuilt. The old gauge board was removed and placed beside a small display about the waterfront. Visitors could see the painted marks, faded by salt and sun. A new sign told them about the remote sensor and the historical measurements. June's name appeared in one paragraph, along with those of others who had made readings before her.

She was invited to the opening. The mayor thanked her for forty years of work. June stood beside the display and felt both proud and slightly fraudulent. She had missed some mornings. She had written figures down too quickly on days when she was ill. There were corrections in red ink throughout the books.

A little girl asked whether the tide would still rise now that the old board was gone.

“Yes,” June said.

“Then why did you measure it?”

June thought of the sailors, the flooded sheds, the old weather patterns and the town's arguments about what was changing. She thought of the technician who had laughed at her question about smell.

“Because we needed to know what the water was doing,” she said. “And because sometimes we were wrong about it.”

The girl accepted this and moved on to a model boat.

That evening June walked home slowly, taking the long way past the harbour. The new sensor blinked on the wall. The tide had begun to rise. A line of foam marked the edge of the water, which was neither high nor low but moving between the two.

She imagined Ben somewhere on another coast, perhaps looking at a different set of marks, perhaps no longer thinking about tides at all. She imagined a person not yet born who would one day read the old notebooks and find them useful, or perhaps find a mistake she had failed to notice. She could not know.

At home she opened her first notebook. On the inside cover, in a younger hand, she had written the date and one instruction: **If uncertain, return to the water.**

She considered adding another sentence. She had learnt much since then, and the old instruction sounded rather more confident than life had turned out to be.

She picked up her pen.

Then she put it down again and sat for a while listening to the wind. The notebook remained open on the table. Tomorrow, if she wished, she could write.

## A Note on Reading Parables

These stories may be read without identifying a lesson. They are not moral equations, and their details are not symbols whose meanings have been officially assigned.

A fictional character may do something admirable for mixed reasons, or something mistaken while trying to help. A reader may favour one course of action while another reader sees a cost that the first overlooked. Both may have noticed something worth examining.

The collection draws on themes in *The Book of Chance*—fortune and hardship, action within uncertainty, shared fortune, fellowship, stewardship and the living world—but it is not a replacement for the founding readings. In particular, fictional incidents cannot prove that chance is metaphysically fundamental, that good deeds return to their authors, or that people can earn favourable outcomes through virtue.

Where a story resembles a real occupation, service, community or natural setting, its details belong to fiction and should not be relied on as practical guidance. Readers may choose to investigate any factual questions separately.

A parable can leave the reader with a disagreement as well as a thought. It can also simply leave a person with the memory of a voice.

## Twelve Questions to Keep

Each question is optional and may be answered privately, discussed with another reader, or left alone.

1. **The Wrong Address:** Which part of Iona's choice mattered most: her extra effort, her respect for privacy, or what she asked her father afterwards?
2. **Two Flood Marks:** When is neighbourly help useful, and when must the systems around people change instead?
3. **The Day the Signal Failed:** Did the interruption create a better conversation, or merely interrupt a difficult one long enough for Rafi to think?
4. **A Quiet Harvest:** How does honest uncertainty differ from an employer asking another person to carry the risk?
5. **The Map Without a Bridge:** What kind of repair does an institution owe after an apparently small error?
6. **The Unmeasured Salt:** When people remember the same relationship differently, what can they offer each other without agreeing?
7. **The Night of No Stars:** When an event fails to deliver its promised experience, what can still be valuable—and what cannot?
8. **The Five O'Clock Ferry:** How should we judge a careful decision when we never know what would have happened otherwise?
9. **The Names Left Off:** How can a record acknowledge those it omitted without pretending to recover all their lives?
10. **The Path Through the Reeds:** Can a design be more accessible to people and more protective of other life, even if neither goal is completely achieved?
11. **The Rules of the Room:** When should a group decline a generous gift? Who bears the cost of that refusal?
12. **What the Tide Keeps:** What kinds of care survive us without requiring anyone to remember our names?
