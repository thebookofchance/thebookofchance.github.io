An invitation, not an entrance exam

Imagine a notice on the wall of a community centre: an evening course is open to anyone. The fee is covered, the teacher is patient and the subject interests you. It looks like an opportunity.

But the buses stop running before the class ends. One person can walk home. Another works a late shift. A third cannot enter the upstairs room. The offer is the same; the possibility it represents is not.

Now imagine that the centre changes the meeting time, makes the room accessible and helps arrange transport. No one can promise that everyone who attends will succeed. Yet something has changed. An opportunity that existed only on a poster has become more available in ordinary life.

This is one way into Confluent Humanism. We begin in circumstances we did not choose. Our choices still matter. When we act together, we can sometimes change the circumstances within which other people must choose.

You do not need to accept a belief about the universe to see the importance of that example. You can explore this philosophy, question it or take something useful from it without agreeing with every part.

1. Why the name?

A confluence is a meeting of streams. A river is shaped by the waters that reach it, the ground it travels through, and what happens along its banks. Our lives are not rivers, but the image is useful: we are shaped by many influences arriving together.

Confluent Humanism is the working name for a developing philosophical outlook associated with the proposed Fellowship of Chance. It considers the meeting of chance, causes, inherited circumstances, experiences and meaningful choices. The word humanism points to our commitment to human dignity and responsible life together. Our concern is not limited to humans: other living beings, ecosystems and the conditions supporting existence have value too.

This is not an established academic school or a scientific theory with settled proofs. It is a philosophy being articulated, tested through reflection and open to criticism. Its central idea is neither that everything happens at random nor that anything can happen if you want it strongly enough.

It is that our lives are shaped by real causes and constraints, yet our actions still have significance. Confluent Humanism also holds a stronger philosophical belief: the future is not completely fixed before it unfolds. That belief is open to argument, not something this guide can prove.

2. What does “chance” mean here?

We use the word chance in more than one way, and it helps to keep those meanings distinct.

First, there is what we do not know. We cannot see every cause or predict every consequence. An unexpected encounter may surprise us even if, in principle, it had causes we could discover.

Second, there are circumstances we did not choose: our place of birth, early relationships, available education, health, inherited resources, or a thousand ordinary conditions. Calling these a matter of fortune is not the same as saying they had no causes. It acknowledges that a person did not personally earn their starting line.

Third, Confluent Humanism holds as a philosophical belief that chance is fundamental to reality and the future is not wholly predetermined. There is an important difference here. In one view, tomorrow is already settled, but nobody can predict it perfectly. In another, tomorrow is not entirely settled, even if we knew everything about today's conditions. Confluent Humanism is committed to the second view. That is a conviction to examine, not something the Fellowship claims science has conclusively proved. How it fits with causation and free will remains an open philosophical question.

Nor is chance treated as a conscious judge. It does not choose favourites, reward generosity or punish people who suffer. A favourable event may inspire gratitude, but an unfavourable event is not evidence that someone lacked faith, effort or worth.

3. Do choices matter if so much is outside our control?

Yes, though not in the simple way we sometimes wish.

You can prepare for an interview and still not receive an offer. You can speak kindly and be misunderstood. You can make a careful decision that leads to a bad result. None of these facts means preparation, kindness or care are pointless. They mean an action's quality and its eventual outcome are not identical.

People have meaningful choices within real limits—what philosophers sometimes call situated agency. We act with the knowledge, habits, energy and opportunities available to us. Those conditions vary. Some can be changed by individual effort; others call for support, better institutions or action together. Saying “just try harder” overlooks what one person may be carrying.

A useful question is not “How do I control my luck?” but “What conditions can I responsibly influence?” Learning a skill may increase the range of work someone can do. Improving access to a building may allow more people to use it. Giving accurate information may prevent someone from wasting a day on a closed door. Sometimes the responsible next action is to seek help, rest or stop doing something that causes harm.

The philosophy refuses two easy answers. One is fatalism: nothing we do changes anything. The other is total control: everyone gets the outcome they deserve. Real lives offer little support for either extreme.

A person's success may involve effort, ability, encouragement and favourable circumstances. Their hardship may involve conditions that cannot be seen from outside. Both observations should temper our judgement of ourselves and others. Our duty to treat people with dignity is a separate ethical commitment: it does not follow automatically from the claim that the universe contains chance.

4. The four foundational statements

The Fellowship's early writings express their outlook in four statements:

1. We exist through chance. We arrive within a world, history and set of circumstances we did not choose. This is an invitation to humility about our starting conditions, not a denial that causes exist.

2. Our actions influence the chances of others. We become part of the conditions other people meet. A word, a decision, a policy or a neglected responsibility may affect lives far beyond our own.

3. Together, we can create possibilities that none of us could create alone. Cooperation may expand what is practical. It also requires fairness: opportunities should not depend solely on who happens to know the right person.

4. When chance favours us, we share what we can. Good fortune can give us room to help. That help may be time, knowledge, care, a thoughtful introduction or material support. It must not come at the cost of basic needs or purchase another person's gratitude, loyalty or beliefs.

These statements give the Fellowship a direction. Imagine neighbours considering a shared garden: one has seeds, another knows the soil, and a third can arrange permission to use an empty plot. Their work may fail, but none could have begun the project in quite the same way alone. Chance brought different circumstances and people together; their choices determined whether anything useful could grow from that meeting.

The statements do not settle every question about what is fair, what we owe one another or when help becomes an intrusion. Those questions call for conversation and judgement.

5. Five values for ordinary days

The philosophy is practised through five complementary values. None is ranked above the others, and none measures whether a person is worthy of belonging.

Hope

Hope makes room for a future that is not fully known. It might support taking one careful step, asking for assistance, or looking for another route. It is not a promise that everything will improve. Sometimes hope means wishing for comfort or companionship when a desired outcome is no longer possible.

Acceptance

Acceptance begins by naming what is real. Something has happened. A boundary exists. We are disappointed, tired, afraid or unsure. Acknowledging that is different from approving an injustice. Acceptance may be the starting point for challenging it.

Reflection

Reflection is the practice of looking carefully at circumstances, choices and consequences. What shaped this day? What did I understand correctly? What did I miss? Reflection connects the other values, but it is not superior to them. It should help us live, not trap us in endless self-examination.

Growth

Growth can be intellectual, emotional, personal or social. Learning to listen, changing an assumption or finding a sustainable way to ask for help may matter as much as gaining a qualification. Rest and stability can be worthwhile. Human dignity is not a productivity target.

Community

Community reminds us that we are neither entirely self-made nor alone in our responsibilities. Offering support can matter even when nothing is returned. Receiving support matters too. Real community allows people to disagree, contribute differently, maintain boundaries and leave without punishment.

The values may pull in different directions. Hope may invite another attempt, while Acceptance asks us to notice exhaustion. Growth may draw us towards study, while Community reminds us of a promise we made. The task is not to win points for a value; it is to make a thoughtful choice under real conditions.

6. Two practices you can try without joining anything

Confluent Humanism suggests two practices that are entirely optional. You may try one, both or neither; having little time, energy or money does not make you less worthy. Neither practice requires an account, purchase, declaration or belief test.

The Reflection of Chance

Choose a quiet moment if you wish. You might write a sentence, think as you walk or talk with someone you trust. Consider:

  • What circumstances shaped my day, including things outside my control?
  • What did I choose, and what have I learned without judging myself by the result alone?
  • What might be worth noticing, preparing for or asking help with next?

You may include gratitude, frustration, loss or uncertainty. You may also decide that today is not a day to reflect. There is no required streak, and private thoughts remain private unless you choose to share them.

The Act of Possibility

Consider one small, manageable action that might widen another person's options or support the living world. It could be sharing reliable public information, asking whether someone wants an introduction, helping make a resource more accessible, or supporting a responsible local environmental effort. Sometimes the right step is to accept offered help yourself or protect the time you need to rest.

Ask before intervening when another person's choices or privacy are involved. If someone does not want an introduction or assistance, respect that answer. Do not promise an outcome, spend money you cannot spare, or expect recognition. An offered act may be declined. That does not make it worthless to have offered thoughtfully.

A philosophy that speaks of possibility must leave room for the reader's choice.

7. Is this a religion, a philosophy or a community?

Confluent Humanism is a developing philosophy. The Fellowship of Chance is the community proposed to explore and practise it, with philosophical and spiritual dimensions. It is not asking readers to leave an existing religion or to adopt a new one. Some people may understand a future Fellowship as religious; others may see it as a philosophical community. The Fellowship does not claim a revealed prophet, a supernatural reward system or a known answer to what happens after death.

People may bring beliefs from their own religious traditions, take a secular view or remain uncertain. They may pray according to their faith, meditate, reflect quietly or do none of these. The Fellowship does not promise that prayer, kindness, donations or membership changes the odds of an event. Different traditions may disagree with parts of the philosophy; that disagreement can be expressed respectfully.

H is the writer and founding steward of the initial texts, using a public name rather than a public personal identity. H is not someone who must be treated as always right; the writings and any future leaders may be questioned and corrected. Privacy cannot exempt leadership from accountability. Before anyone exercises formal authority over members, complaints or money, the Fellowship would need clear public rules, safe ways to raise concerns and checks by people who are independent of H or any leader involved. These safeguards are requirements for a future organisation, not systems already in operation.

The Fellowship itself remains in development. Its public writings can be read now, but a registered community, governing Council, local groups, mentoring and hardship assistance are not yet operating. Under the current draft plan, organised membership would be free and initially limited to adults aged 18 and over. This proposed age boundary concerns organised participation and safeguarding, not who may read or question the books. Anyone may explore the writings without signing up. Before membership opens, the Fellowship would also need agreed rules for respectful conduct, privacy, safety and governance.

This distinction matters. A book can offer a thoughtful direction before an organisation is ready to deliver its ambitions. Readers deserve to know the difference.

8. Questions a thoughtful reader may ask

If chance is fundamental, does that prove free will? No. Confluent Humanism holds that meaningful choices and an open future belong together, but the relationship between chance, causes and free will needs further argument. Randomness alone does not explain responsibility.

Must I believe the future is genuinely undetermined to read these books? No. You may find the ethical commitments useful while questioning the claim about reality. A future membership agreement is proposed to cover respectful conduct and safety; the final rules have not yet been adopted.

If our beginnings are unequal, is effort meaningless? No. Effort matters, and so do material conditions and support. The point is to recognise both, rather than blaming a person for every outcome or treating opportunity as equally available to everyone.

Does being fortunate mean I owe everyone everything? No. The call to share is bounded by capacity, consent, foreseeable consequences and fairness. You may protect your own wellbeing and responsibilities. You can also be a person who needs help.

Does the universe reward good actions? The philosophy does not claim that it does. A kind act may be worth doing even when no reward appears and no one remembers the giver.

Can I disagree with the writings or with H? Yes. Criticism is compatible with thoughtful participation. Membership, should it become available, would involve fair conduct rules rather than compulsory agreement with every metaphysical proposition.

Is care for nature part of the philosophy? Yes. The value of life and of ecosystems extends beyond how useful they are to people. What particular duties follow may require difficult discussion and attention to evidence.

What happens after death? The Fellowship does not claim to know. Readers are free to bring their own convictions while distinguishing faith, hope and evidence.

9. Where to go next

There is no required reading order. Choose the route that suits your interest:

  • To understand the philosophy in depth: Begin with The Book of Chance, especially the introduction and the readings on the open future and shared fortune.
  • To try something practical: Start with The Five Values or one private reflection using a journal or worksheet. You can stop whenever you like.
  • To explore through stories or discussion: Read The Parables of Chance for fiction, or Conversations of Chance for questions to consider alone or with others.

If you want more on everyday opportunities and barriers, The Little Book of Possibilities expands that theme. The Letters from H explore individual ethical questions. No path makes someone a better reader.

The current collection is available through The Book of Chance website. The site's Library brings the released works together for free reading and download.

You may finish this guide with a question rather than an answer. You may conclude that chance means something different to you. You may carry away only the practical thought that people do not begin from equal conditions, and that a thoughtful action can sometimes widen another life.

That is enough to begin a conversation.

Through chance, we meet. Through choice, we grow. Together, we create possibility.

— H
