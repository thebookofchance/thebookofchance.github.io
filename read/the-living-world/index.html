Reading X of XII

The Living World

Before there were witnesses

The world did not begin when a human being first described it. Oceans moved without our names for them. Forests grew and changed. Creatures fed, travelled, sheltered and died. The conditions that would eventually make human life possible emerged through histories no person planned.

We belong to these histories. We do not stand outside them, choosing the world as though it were a collection of materials placed at our disposal.

This is easy to forget in ordinary life. We speak of land as property, water as supply, animals as resources and the seasons as useful or inconvenient. These words describe real relationships and needs. People must eat, build shelter and care for families. Yet a vocabulary of use can make everything beyond our immediate purposes appear empty of value.

Confluent Humanism holds that existence beyond humanity matters in its own right. This is an ethical commitment defended through considerations of welfare, relationship and continuity; the mere presence of chance in nature cannot establish it. We have reasons to attend to several kinds of value. Creatures capable of suffering have interests in avoiding pain and injury, irrespective of whether anyone is watching. Living things and ecological relationships can matter as parts of a flourishing world even when their importance is not easily translated into an individual creature's experience. And future people and other lives will inherit the conditions our decisions leave behind.

These reasons are related but not identical. The welfare of an animal, the continuity of a habitat and the interests of a future community may point toward different duties. Recognising such differences helps us weigh them without reducing the whole living world to property or pretending that every species, river and person makes the same sort of claim.

The claim does not settle every question. It does not say that a person and a forest have identical interests, or that we should never alter an environment. It asks us to begin from a more attentive position: the living world is not made significant only when it becomes useful to us.

A bank beside the river

Consider a river passing through a town. Children grow up recognising its bends. Birds nest along the banks. Fish travel through its waters. Trees hold the soil in place, and people have built homes, paths and businesses nearby.

In this invented thought experiment, a council proposes works to protect the town from flooding. Suppose, for the story's purposes, that the risk of another damaging flood is credible, but that the exact effects of competing designs are uncertain. People's safety matters. The easiest construction would also remove much of the vegetation along one bank. These assumptions invite an ethical discussion, not an engineering conclusion about any real river.

Different people speak. Some fear for their homes. Others worry about the wildlife and the future of the river. A few ask whether alternatives have been studied. Nobody can simply wave away the problem, because both safety and ecological harm are real considerations.

The proposal divides the meeting. A resident whose home has flooded twice asks why the council is still debating. A person who fishes downstream wonders whether removing the bank's vegetation will alter the river in ways nobody has yet examined. A worker points out that an alternative design may cost more and take longer. A family living on the lowest street cannot afford another flood.

The council cannot choose by asking which side cares more. It needs to ask who is exposed to immediate harm, who will pay, whose knowledge has been heard, who holds rights or decision-making authority over the place, what protection the designs are reasonably expected to provide and what ecological costs each may impose. Consultation does not replace legally required participation, consent or other obligations; these depend on the people and jurisdiction concerned. It should ask whether temporary measures could protect homes while longer-term evidence is gathered, whether a less destructive design is feasible, and whether affected residents can challenge the decision.

Let us assume, for the sake of the thought experiment, that credible assessment identifies an urgent risk to homes and no timely alternative that offers equivalent protection. Some intervention may then be justified despite ecological loss. Even then the choice should minimise avoidable damage, specify what will be monitored, provide for mitigation or restoration where feasible, and state publicly who is accountable if the effects differ from expectations. If, on the other hand, the less damaging design can protect residents at a reasonable and bearable extra cost, choosing the cheapest destructive route merely because the loss falls on wildlife and future communities would be hard to defend.

The judgement may change with the evidence. A livelihood threatened by delay, a vulnerable species, a flooded hospital or a workable alternative could alter the conclusion. No value-neutral calculation can settle every competing consideration, and consensus may remain impossible. A decision must nevertheless be made, with reasons that those bearing the greatest costs have been allowed to scrutinise.

This is an invented deliberation, not a technical flood-management recommendation. Its point is that Acceptance of real danger, Hope for better alternatives, Reflection on evidence, Growth in institutional learning and Community across human and non-human lives can guide an actual decision without guaranteeing a painless one.

An ethic of belonging

It is possible to care about the natural world for reasons that are partly practical. Clean water, fertile ground and stable habitats support human life. Damage to them often places heavy burdens on people who have the least influence over the decisions that caused it.

But practical dependence is not the whole argument. We can recognise beauty, complexity and the lives of other creatures without first calculating a human benefit. A bird is not simply a source of pleasure for an observer. A stand of trees is not only future timber or a measure of carbon. Other forms of life have histories and ways of existing that matter even when we cannot fully understand them.

This does not demand that every person abandon the ordinary activities through which they live. Food, transport, homes and healthcare have environmental effects. Moral concern must be balanced with human need, available alternatives and the harms that different decisions may cause. An ethic that ignores poverty, disability or the burdens of transition will struggle to be fair.

The responsibility may therefore fall differently across a community. A person with very little control over their circumstances should not bear the same demands as an institution able to redesign a harmful process. People who benefit most from a decision may owe greater attention to the costs carried by others, including those not yet born.

Disagreement remains. Someone may give special weight to the suffering of sentient animals; another may stress the integrity of an ecosystem that supports many forms of life. A conservation proposal might protect the survival of native species while requiring the removal or killing of individual animals introduced from elsewhere. The concern for an ecosystem and the concern for each animal's suffering can pull in different directions. No universal rule has been settled here for such cases. One must examine the evidence, the available alternatives, the scale of suffering, responsibilities to affected communities and what might happen if no action is taken. Giving the living world moral standing changes the questions a responsible decision-maker must answer. It cannot save us from answering them.

The limits of foresight

Environmental care confronts us with the very difficulty that lies at the centre of this book: we act without knowing all the consequences. A species may respond unexpectedly to a change in habitat. A local intervention may help in one place and cause difficulties elsewhere. The future of a landscape cannot be commanded by intention alone.

Uncertainty does not excuse carelessness. It gives us reasons to examine evidence, listen to appropriate expertise, consider reversible options where possible, and review the results of what we do. Scientific knowledge matters here.

So do the observations and responsibilities of communities whose lives are bound to particular places. Their authority may derive from law, enduring stewardship, collective rights or knowledge developed over generations; it cannot be honoured by a ceremonial invitation to speak after every important decision has already been made. We should ask who is entitled to decide, whose consent is necessary where applicable, and whose work of care is being overlooked. We should neither speak on behalf of an Indigenous tradition nor present its diverse teachings as if they were one convenient illustration of our own philosophy. Claims about a specific place require careful study and respectful engagement with the people concerned.

The absence of perfect knowledge should not become a permanent excuse for delay where serious preventable harm is foreseeable. Equally, urgency should not prevent reasonable scrutiny of a proposed solution. We need the humility to revise a plan when the world answers differently from what we expected.

There is a difference between saying that chance shapes the world and saying that the consequences of our actions are beyond our concern. We can alter conditions. We can reduce some dangers and increase others. We should therefore consider not only what we hope to gain but what may be placed at risk.

The lives we do not meet

Much of our ethical attention is directed toward people whose faces we can see. We know the friend who asks for help. We may know the neighbour who needs a lift. It is harder to imagine responsibility toward a future child, an unknown community or a creature whose life will never touch ours directly.

Yet our actions may become part of their starting conditions. A damaged waterway can outlast the decision that harmed it. A restored habitat may support life beyond the lifetimes of those who worked on it. Knowledge passed on can help someone not yet born avoid a mistake we have already made.

This wider view of Community does not diminish the importance of human relationships. It enlarges the circle of concern in which those relationships take place.

We inherited a living world that made our arrival possible. We will leave conditions for lives that follow. Our responsibility is not to preserve everything unchanged, as if change itself were a wrong. It is to act with care toward what exists, what depends upon it and what may yet come to be.

For reflection

  • What part of the living world do I encounter so often that I have stopped noticing it?
  • Who or what bears the environmental costs of a choice from which I benefit?
  • When a difficult trade-off arises, whose interests are being counted and whose are absent?

An optional practice: Spend time attending to a nearby living place without trying to turn the experience into a task. If a specific need becomes clear, consider a proportionate, informed action that respects both local people and the wider living world.
