The Book of Chance

Introduction — Before Your First Choice

A child is born in the early hours of a morning. Somewhere beyond the hospital walls, traffic moves through familiar streets. People have missed trains, changed jobs, postponed journeys, answered messages and failed to answer others. Inside the room, the child opens their eyes upon a world already in progress.

The child has made no plan. Yet much has already been decided around them.

There is a family, or there is no family able to remain. There is a home with warmth, or one in which warmth must be found elsewhere. There may be books, quiet, medical care, patience and time. There may be noise, uncertainty, hunger or danger. None of these conditions is a reward for a decision the child has made.

We tend to remember the parts of our lives that appear to belong to us: the examination passed, the friendship pursued, the difficult conversation finally begun. Those decisions matter. But the story that makes them possible extends far beyond us. A teacher stayed after class. A neighbour offered a lift. A law changed. A bridge held. A storm took another route. Someone met someone else years before we were born.

Some influences are obvious. We know who taught us to read, or who gave us a place to stay. Others disappear into the background. We may never discover why we encountered one person rather than another, or what would have happened had we made a different turn on an ordinary afternoon.

We live among causes we can study and events we can influence. Confluent Humanism’s deeper claim—that the future itself is not wholly settled—will receive a serious examination in Reading IV. We can begin here with a simpler observation, available to people who disagree about the universe: inherited circumstances matter, and what someone does may change the conditions another person faces.

An unsettled future does not erase the difference between a careful choice and a careless one. The child who later learns a language will hear conversations differently from someone who never had the opportunity to learn. A community that builds a safer crossing changes the risks borne by those who use it. Actions have consequences even when consequences cannot be guaranteed.

Nor are all futures equally accessible. An open future is not an unlimited one. Money, health, discrimination, physical laws, other people's decisions and countless circumstances can close possibilities as surely as effort may open them. The sentence “anything is possible” can comfort a person for a moment, but it may also become cruel when used to deny the barriers they face.

The more careful claim is this: a person's present circumstances do not tell the whole story of what may follow, and their actions may make a real difference to what becomes possible. Sometimes they cannot act alone. Sometimes the necessary change is not in the person at all, but in the world around them.

This is where the philosophy becomes an ethical question.

We inherit conditions shaped partly by other people. We, in turn, become part of the conditions others inherit. A school that welcomes a child struggling to settle in changes something. A community that makes its meeting place accessible changes something. A friend who listens without judgement may not solve a problem, but may make it easier to ask for help.

No choice controls every consequence. Yet the fact that outcomes escape us does not make our care, negligence, courage or refusal irrelevant. What we can reasonably foresee and what we choose to do with that knowledge still matter.

Throughout this book we will return to five values: hope, acceptance, reflection, growth and community. They will sometimes pull in different directions. Hope without acceptance can refuse to see reality. Acceptance without hope can become resignation. Reflection can deepen understanding or turn into endless self-reproach. Growth can widen a life or shrink it into a catalogue of achievements. Community can sustain a person or overwhelm them with demands. None is sufficient alone.

The book's invitation is to practise them together, with discernment.

You can begin without agreeing with every proposition. Begin, instead, with a question.

What had to happen before you could make your first choice? And now that you are here, what can your choices make possible for someone else?
