Reading I of XII

Arrival

The ground beneath our feet

Before your first choice, there was a world.

It had already been shaped by the choices of people you would never meet. Roads had been laid or neglected. Languages had developed, laws had been written, and institutions had gained or lost the trust of those who depended upon them. There were customs whose reasons had been forgotten, knowledge handed down through generations, and injustices whose effects remained long after the people responsible had died.

You entered this world not as an empty possibility but as a particular life. Your body came with abilities and limits. Your home, if you had one, stood in a particular place. The people around you had histories of their own. Some had opportunities they could pass on. Others were struggling to keep what little they had.

We do not choose the circumstances of our birth. Yet beginnings matter.

A child who feels safe may find it easier to attempt something unfamiliar. Another child may learn to be watchful before learning to be curious. One is surrounded by books; another discovers them only when a teacher notices. One family knows someone who can explain how an application works. Another receives the same form and wonders where to begin.

These differences do not determine every future. People sometimes move far beyond the circumstances in which they began. Others suffer despite every advantage. But to recognise the influence of starting conditions is to refuse a comforting falsehood: that every person starts on the same line and merely runs with different determination.

We often tell stories about success from the point at which achievement becomes visible. We describe a person's discipline, courage, persistence or talent. These may be entirely real. What disappears from the account is the time someone else gave, the door someone else opened, the freedom to make mistakes without losing everything, or the good fortune of encountering the right opportunity when preparation had finally become possible.

To notice those conditions is not to diminish the achievement. It is to tell a fuller story.

The two gardens

Two neighbours planted seeds from the same packet. Both took care to read the instructions. Both watered their beds and removed the weeds.

One garden had deep soil, shelter from the wind and steady sunlight. The other lay above stone, beside a broken gutter that rattled in the wind and sent rainwater across the seedlings each time a storm arrived.

At harvest, the first neighbour had baskets to carry home. The second gathered only a little.

“Patience and effort,” said the first neighbour, pleased with the result.

The second looked at their small harvest. “I had those too.”

The following season, the neighbours repaired the gutter and carried compost to the harder ground. The difference between their gardens did not disappear. The second grew more than before, and the first began to understand that a good harvest could be both earned and helped by the ground beneath it.

What the story does not settle

Neither neighbour can explain the harvest by effort alone. The story raises a harder question: when we judge an outcome, how should we distinguish a person's choices from conditions we have not taken time to see?

We should therefore be careful when judging other people. A life resists any explanation offered in a single word.

If someone prospers, we can acknowledge their work while remaining curious about what supported it. If someone struggles, we can ask what obstacles they faced before declaring what they should have done. Where harm has occurred, compassion need not prevent investigation or responsibility. Where an unfair system has made a good outcome less likely, helping an individual is worthwhile, but changing the system may be necessary as well.

Consider education. Telling a young person that study may improve future opportunities is sensible. Telling them that qualifications guarantee a secure life is not. The usefulness of a course depends on its quality, the learner's circumstances, the world of work, health, geography, timing and other people. Preparing carefully may improve a person's prospects while leaving the outcome uncertain.

This may be described as increasing one's chances: building capacities, removing obstacles and making useful options more accessible. The idea of judging opportunity by what people can actually do, rather than by resources alone, has also been developed in the philosophical capability approach. Neither account requires believing that luck can be stored or purchased.

A person who has inherited favourable conditions has no reason to be ashamed of receiving them. They may take pride in making good use of them. But gratitude becomes more honest when it acknowledges that others did not all begin with the same resources.

A person who has inherited difficult conditions is not defined by them. Neither is that person obliged to make hardship meaningful for the comfort of an observer. Their life has worth before a single obstacle is overcome.

This is a central commitment of Confluent Humanism: that we treat human dignity as independent of fortune.

Why give each person that standing? Consider what it would mean to make worth depend upon an outcome. The child born into comfort would begin with moral credit never earned. The child born into danger would begin in moral debt. A person whose health deteriorates, or whose work becomes impossible, would lose standing precisely when consideration is most necessary. Such a rule would be difficult to justify fairly to the people it disadvantages.

Human lives depend, in different ways, upon protections, patience and opportunities whose need we did not choose. Yet dignity cannot be a bargain offered only to those able to return the favour. A newborn, someone who cannot work, and someone who will never be able to repay assistance have the same moral standing as the most productive person in the room. Our shared vulnerability helps us recognise why. If dignity is genuinely equal, regard for a person cannot depend upon their ability to return a favour. This principle asks whether the rules governing us can be defended to those who bear their costs.

This is a moral argument, not a fact revealed by the workings of chance. Other ethical traditions defend dignity in other ways. The task is to give reasons for dignity—and to act accordingly—rather than to make anyone’s worth depend upon winning a more fortunate life.

What we pass onward

We cannot choose our beginnings. We can, however, take part in shaping someone else's conditions.

A patient explanation may make a subject less frightening. A recommendation, made honestly, may help a capable person be considered for work. A shared tool, a lift to an appointment, a quiet room in which to study, a change to an inaccessible process—none is spectacular. Each may alter what another person can attempt.

This should not become an obligation to fix every disadvantage we notice. No individual holds enough time, influence or money for that task. Sometimes the responsible act is recognising our limits. Sometimes it is joining others so that a burden does not fall on one person alone.

The purpose of remembering our beginnings is not to keep score of who received more. It is to ask what a fair chance might require. Equal respect does not always mean identical help. Repairing the broken gutter in the parable gives one gardener something the other already possessed; withholding that repair in the name of treating both alike would preserve an unfair difference.

Still, we should ask the people affected what would help. An outsider's idea of fairness may overlook the cost, dignity or preferences of those meant to benefit.

The ground beneath us is never the whole story. It is still part of the story, and it is ground we can sometimes improve for those who come after us.

For reflection

  • Which opportunities reached me before I recognised them as opportunities?
  • Where have I judged another person's outcome without seeing their starting conditions?
  • What is one condition I could help improve for someone else, without assuming I know what they need?

An optional practice: Recall one person, place or resource that made something easier for you. If it is appropriate and welcome, offer a similarly specific form of support to someone else. If your circumstances do not allow that now, recognition itself is a beginning.
