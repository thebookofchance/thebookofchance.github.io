Reading II of XII

The Unfolding

Lives at their intersections

A journey rarely has only one beginning.

When someone tells us how they came to live in a certain city, enter a profession or meet a friend, they choose a place to start. They might begin with an application, a conversation or a decision to leave home. But another account could begin earlier: with the person who mentioned the opportunity, the family that could afford the journey, or the change in circumstances that made departure necessary.

Go back far enough and any life becomes a meeting of other lives. The intersections multiply beyond our ability to follow them. Some are intimate and enduring; others last only a moment.

We often regard these moments as insignificant until we discover what followed them. A postponed trip leads to a conversation. A wrong turn leads to a new place. A recommendation arrives at the right time for a person who has been trying for months. Another recommendation arrives too late.

An explanation assembled after an event may not have been available before it. That difference tells us about our knowledge, not by itself about whether the event was fundamentally undetermined. Nor does it show that the event was meant to occur. The meaning we give an experience and the cause of that experience are different questions.

The missed bus

A traveller hurried toward a bus stop and reached it just as the bus moved away. Frustrated, they sat down to wait for the next one.

Another person was waiting there. They began to talk. The stranger mentioned a workshop nearby; the traveller attended it a week later and eventually formed a lasting friendship with someone they met there.

Years passed. At a gathering, a friend heard the story and smiled.

“So missing that bus was a blessing.”

“It became part of something good,” the traveller replied. “But I don't know what the other journey would have held.”

The roads we cannot compare

In ordinary conversation we sometimes say, “Everything happens for a reason.” The phrase can express comfort. It can also imply that suffering was necessary for some later benefit. Confluent Humanism does not make that claim.

A missed bus may lead to friendship. It may also lead to a missed appointment. The same event may be trivial to one person and costly to another. We should be wary of a philosophy that declares a misfortune good simply because someone eventually found a way to live beyond it.

The point here is less a theory of the universe than a warning about the stories we tell ourselves. Memory arranges scattered events into a line that looks inevitable from the far end. We forget the invitations declined, the choices that were nearly made, and the accidents that left no visible trace. A coherent life story may help us understand ourselves. It becomes misleading when it claims that every turn was necessary.

Within that uncertainty, agency matters. A conversation can offer a possibility; choosing whether to follow it remains the traveller's decision. But even decisions are shaped by experience. Someone who has learned to trust their judgement may recognise an opportunity another person overlooks. Someone who has been hurt may reasonably hesitate. Learning changes how we notice, choose and act.

People can weigh reasons, learn and answer for what they do. Their ability to act on a decision also depends upon safety and available options. We choose within conditions we did not create alone; our choices then become part of the world others encounter.

A chance encounter is not a substitute for effort, either. The traveller in the parable still chose to attend the workshop and later spent time building a friendship. A meeting can make a path visible without walking it for us. Equally, the absence of such a meeting can leave a capable person unaware of a path that others take for granted.

This is why an opportunity should never be confused with a promise. We can prepare to recognise openings, and we can make some openings fairer and more visible to others. We cannot write the unseen alternatives into a history that never occurred.

What a meeting makes possible

The word confluence describes what happens when different streams come together. It does not imply that the streams become identical. In human life, people arrive with different experiences, interests and obligations. One person can teach a skill. Another can offer a perspective that changes how a problem is understood. A third may simply listen carefully enough for someone else to find words for their own next step.

An older neighbour has recently moved to a city where the official letters arrive in a language they are still learning. A younger neighbour helps them work out which office can answer a question, without pretending to know the answer. Weeks later, the older neighbour repairs the younger one's torn jacket, not to settle an account but because the two have become friends. Neither assistance tells us which life is more capable. Their knowledge belongs to different places and different years.

No one is required to convert every encounter into an opportunity. Friendship is not networking in disguise. The people we meet are not resources waiting to be used. Sometimes the worth of an encounter lies in companionship, attention or delight rather than any measurable advantage.

But because lives touch, what we do in one place may have effects elsewhere. A kind word may give a person confidence to try again. A careless remark may narrow their sense of what is possible. Often we will never learn which effect, if any, our words had. This uncertainty is part of the reason to act with care, not a reason to claim invisible achievements.

Confluence asks us to notice both humility and responsibility. We do not see the whole pattern; we are still part of it.

When we look back at the turns our lives have taken, we can honour the good without making a myth of inevitability. When we look forward, we can prepare without pretending preparation purchases an outcome.

The road not taken is unknown. The road before us is not yet settled. What we do at the next intersection still matters.

For reflection

  • What encounter altered an opportunity I could recognise at the time—or only much later?
  • Where have I turned an uncertain sequence of events into a story of inevitability?
  • What could I learn now that might help me notice a possibility I would otherwise miss?

An optional practice: If someone would genuinely benefit from information you possess, ask whether they would like it. Offer it without pressure or expectation that it will lead to anything further.
