Reading III of XII

Fortune and Hardship

The difference between a decision and its result

A person can make a thoughtful decision and suffer. Another can act carelessly and escape harm. It is tempting to read the result backward as though the outcome contains a complete account of the choice.

When something goes well, we naturally look for reasons. A plan worked. A person prepared. Someone took a risk at the right moment. These explanations may be useful. They become misleading when we conclude that every good result proves good judgement, or every bad result proves failure of character.

Chance can spare an error. It can also undo careful work. The difference matters wherever we judge ourselves and others.

A worker who follows sound safety procedures may still be injured when a rare combination of events overwhelms existing precautions. Another worker may ignore a rule and suffer no immediate consequence. If we judge only by what happened, we may praise the careless and blame the careful. A wiser response asks what each person knew, what options existed, whether the procedures were adequate, and what can be improved before the next decision.

The distinction makes responsibility more exacting. We should judge a decision by the evidence, duties and foreseeable risks that existed when it was made, while investigating a harmful outcome for what needs repair. These are related questions, but they are not identical.

The crossing

Two builders constructed bridges across a river. Each inspected the banks, chose materials suited to the task and worked carefully with the knowledge available.

Then an unexpected flood came. One bridge remained. The other was carried away.

At first the town praised the surviving builder and blamed the other. The standing bridge seemed to tell a simple story about skill, and the fallen bridge about failure.

But when people examined the river, they found that its banks and currents had responded differently. They also discovered where both bridges might be strengthened against future floods.

They stopped treating survival alone as proof of wisdom. They repaired what could be repaired, and they changed what they had learned to change.

Learning without a verdict

The town does not simply call the flood bad luck. It investigates. If a builder ignored a known hazard, that matters. If the flood exceeded what anyone could reasonably have anticipated, that matters too.

When we consider another person's decision, fairness requires us to take seriously the information and choices available at the time. Hindsight is a powerful illusion. Once we know the outcome, it can be difficult to remember how many outcomes still appeared possible before it occurred.

The same care should shape how we speak to people after a loss.

“Everything happens for a reason” may be offered kindly. “What did you learn?” may sound constructive. Neither is always welcome. Someone who has just lost a job, a relationship, a home or a loved one may not be ready to turn grief into a lesson. Some experiences do not become good because they can eventually be spoken about with clarity.

It is possible to learn from a painful event without claiming that the pain was necessary. It is possible to appreciate what followed a loss without being grateful for the loss itself. When people are harmed, the question of prevention may be urgent even if the question of personal meaning has no answer.

Acceptance begins with telling the truth about what has occurred. It is not approval. A person experiencing discrimination can accept that the discrimination happened while refusing to accept it as just or permanent. A community can acknowledge that hardship exists while organising to change the conditions producing it.

There are times when asking someone to try harder becomes a way of refusing to see those conditions. A person may already be working every available hour. Another may be carrying responsibilities invisible to an outsider. Advice to “keep improving your chances” is inadequate if it ignores illness, exhaustion, economic insecurity or a system arranged against the person receiving it.

A commitment to growth must therefore live beside commitments to rest, support and structural change. Increasing opportunity is not always an exercise in self-improvement. Sometimes it means ensuring that a person is safe enough to recover. Sometimes it means correcting an unfair rule, sharing information, or offering practical help without asking the recipient to prove they deserve it.

Two risks, two outcomes

Imagine two drivers leaving separate gatherings on a wet evening. Each is distracted, drives too quickly near a crossing and fails to give the road the attention it requires. The first reaches home without incident. The second strikes a pedestrian, who is injured.

It would be a mistake to call the first driver responsible simply because nobody was hurt. Both took an unreasonable risk. The second, however, must now face a harm that actually occurred. The pedestrian may need urgent care, rehabilitation, transport and practical help. Someone must attend to those needs, investigate the event and consider what reparation is possible. Additional duties arising from actual harm do not necessarily mean the second driver had a worse character than the first. Nor does treating the two choices as equally reckless mean treating the two outcomes as though they caused equal loss.

There is no effortless calculation here. What a person morally owes, what an insurer or institution must provide, and what the law requires are related but distinct questions. Those affected should not be forced to wait for a debate about blame before receiving appropriate care. The drivers' actions call for judgement; the injury calls for a response.

Philosophers sometimes call the difficulty of assessing people when outcomes depend partly on luck moral luck. We need not solve the entire debate to see three separate tasks: evaluate the care with which a choice was made, address the harm that followed, and change conditions to reduce the risk of its happening again. The tasks inform one another. None replaces the others.

The dignity that remains

Fortune is not a measure of worth. A lottery winner has not been shown to be morally superior. A person who loses a home in a disaster has not failed a hidden examination. We may speak of a fortunate outcome as a blessing of chance, but the phrase expresses wonder or gratitude, not a belief that chance selected someone for special treatment.

This matters just as much when fortune is ours.

Success can make our own decisions look more inevitable than they were. We remember courage and hard work, sometimes forgetting support, timing, luck and the risks someone else absorbed. To recognise those influences is not to apologise for succeeding. It can make gratitude more accurate and generosity less patronising.

Hardship can make our mistakes feel larger than they were. A person can acknowledge poor judgement without making a single decision the final definition of their life. There may be repair to attempt and obligations to meet. There may also be circumstances that could not reasonably have been controlled.

We do not have to remove the difference between actions to preserve the equal dignity of people. Some decisions are better informed, more generous or less harmful than others. Some people have a duty to make amends. But a person's human worth is not awarded or withdrawn by the outcome of a single chapter.

A useful response to uncertainty is neither automatic blame nor indifference. It is an honest assessment of what happened, what could reasonably have been known, what harm needs attention and what might make a better decision possible next time.

And sometimes, before all that, the useful response is to sit beside someone and say, “I am here.”

For reflection

  • Have I mistaken a favourable result for proof that a decision was sound, or an unfavourable result for proof that it was careless?
  • When have I demanded a lesson from an experience that first needed grief, rest or support?
  • What can be improved—in a decision, a relationship or a system—without humiliating the person affected?

An optional practice: If someone shares a setback, ask what kind of response they want: listening, practical help, another perspective or simply company. Do not assume their loss exists to teach either of you a lesson.
