Reading V of XII

Action Within Uncertainty

The difference an action makes

A person stands outside a room where a difficult conversation is about to begin. They have rehearsed what they want to say. They have tried to imagine the other person's response and considered whether today is the right day. Their hands are unsteady. They could leave, wait, ask someone to accompany them, or enter.

No choice offers a complete view of what follows.

We often delay action because we want more certainty than a situation can provide. Sometimes delay is wise. We may need information, safety, money, rest or time to understand what we are asking of another person. At other times, the wish for certainty becomes a demand that the world promise us a result before we begin.

Confluent Humanism does not teach that people must always act. It asks us to distinguish what is within our influence from what remains outside it, and to respond with whatever judgement and support are available.

Preparation is an action. So is asking for help. Refusing an unsafe demand can be an action of considerable courage. Waiting may be a form of care when someone is exhausted or when more information is likely to change the decision. The value of a choice cannot be reduced to how decisive it looks from a distance.

When we act, we become part of the circumstances from which later events emerge. We do not control all their consequences, but neither are we merely spectators.

The lantern

A group walked a dark path. One traveller said that a lantern could not remove every stone or show every turn, so there was little point carrying one.

Another lit a small lamp.

They still stumbled. The light reached only a short distance ahead, and the path sometimes changed beyond its reach. But they could see where the ground was broken, when to slow down, and where another traveller needed a hand.

By morning they had not conquered the darkness. They had travelled with greater care because they had brought the light they possessed.

A light with limits

A little light can reveal a hazard without making the path safe. Knowledge is useful in the same way; we must still ask what is missing, who bears the risks and whether the person receiving advice has the means to follow it.

Consider a young adult deciding whether to move for work. The promise of a new job may be meaningful, but so are rent, family commitments, access to healthcare, the reliability of the offer and the cost of returning home if the position disappears. Advice from someone with savings and several safe alternatives may be sincere yet poorly suited to someone without them.

A thoughtful choice does not require eliminating all risk; that may be impossible. It does require understanding which risks are acceptable, which are imposed unfairly, and which could be reduced through preparation or mutual support.

Three questions can be useful before a difficult step. What do I know well enough to act upon? Who will bear the cost if I am wrong? What could make the decision safer, more reversible or easier to revise? These are not a universal formula. A person under pressure may have little time to answer them; a person with authority may have a duty to take the time.

Consider someone invited to a community art class. Before they can decide freely, they may need to know whether the venue is accessible, what materials cost and whether attendance can be flexible. An open invitation becomes more meaningful when the practical conditions are clear.

This is one reason collective action matters. Imagine several night-shift workers who notice that the same hazardous schedule is leaving people exhausted. Each has tried to cope alone, and each has been told to be more careful. Together they record what is happening, seek informed advice and ask for the roster and rest provisions to change. Their first request is refused. They must decide what risk they can bear, whether to organise further and who can safely speak. There is no neat ending to this imagined example. It is nevertheless a different kind of agency from individual vigilance: a collective attempt to change the conditions under which future choices will be made.

A student may not overcome an inaccessible course by studying harder. A family facing flood damage may need more than advice about planning. Systems can be challenged, resources pooled and barriers removed. The lamp may be held by one person, but the path is shared.

When an action causes harm

The fact that our choices are shaped by circumstance does not remove responsibility for their effects. A person who harms another may have suffered, learned destructive habits or acted under pressure. These conditions matter to understanding what occurred and how harm might be prevented. They do not make the person harmed less deserving of protection.

Responsibility should be proportionate to what an individual knew, could reasonably foresee and was able to choose. It should also include the willingness to acknowledge what happened and take part in repair when repair is possible. An apology cannot demand forgiveness. An explanation cannot purchase exemption from consequences.

We should apply the same attention to decisions made by groups and institutions. It is easy for responsibility to disappear when every person claims to have performed only a small task. Yet a policy, a procedure or a design decision may expose others to harm. A community that values reflection must examine the patterns it creates, not only the intentions it celebrates.

There will be times when no option is free from cost. Caring for one person may mean disappointing another. Taking an opportunity may mean giving up a different commitment. Refusing a request may protect needed rest while leaving someone else to find support. Ethical life does not consist of always selecting the painless path. It consists of trying to see those affected, recognising our limits and being prepared to reconsider our choices.

A decision that cannot please everyone

Suppose someone who volunteers as a mentor has promised to help a newcomer prepare for an important interview. That evening, an older relative needs care; the mentor is also exhausted after work. Keeping the appointment would mean leaving the relative without adequate support or working past the point of reasonable safety. Cancelling may disappoint someone who has few other sources of guidance.

All five values have something to say. Community asks the mentor to take the newcomer's need seriously. Acceptance acknowledges the limits of one body and one evening. Reflection asks what was promised and whether the conflict might have been anticipated. Growth includes the newcomer learning to prepare and the mentor learning better boundaries. Hope asks whether another route remains possible. The values illuminate the problem; none supplies a mechanical answer.

A responsible response would begin by checking whether the relative is safe, then speaking promptly and honestly to the newcomer. The mentor might offer a short telephone call if feasible, recommend a reliable preparation resource, or—with permission—ask another suitable mentor to help. If no alternative exists, the mentor may have to cancel. The newcomer's disappointment would still deserve acknowledgement. It would not make the mentor's limits a moral failure.

A different detail could change the judgement. If the relative had another carer and the appointment concerned an immediate, life-changing deadline, a brief meeting might be reasonable. A promise matters, but so does who bears the consequences of fulfilling it. The answer depends on real obligations, risks, consent and available alternatives. Good ethical reasoning can lead to a choice that remains painful.

The aim is neither to sacrifice the mentor nor to dismiss the newcomer. It is to choose proportionately, explain the decision, make whatever repair is possible and learn how not to make such conflicts routine.

Courage without a guarantee

It can be difficult to act when we know we may fail. We want to believe that effort will be honoured by an outcome, that a careful plan will be rewarded, that a generous action will return to us in some form. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it does not.

We have good reason to judge the wisdom of an action by more than luck alone. A person who prepares thoroughly for an interview and is rejected may still have acted well. Someone who declines an unsafe arrangement may lose a chance they wanted and still have made the responsible decision. A community that works to improve access may not succeed in its first attempt, but its members can examine their methods without treating the attempt as worthless.

There is a temptation to turn this into a new instruction: make every setback productive. That would misunderstand it. Some failures need rest. Some losses deserve anger. Some situations are so constrained that the first ethical task belongs to those with greater power to change them.

Action within uncertainty is not a demand to keep moving. It is the recognition that deliberate steps, pauses and refusals can each alter the conditions in which later choices will be made.

The lantern lights only part of the path. That may be enough to take a considered step. It may also be enough to see that we should stop and help one another find another way.

For reflection

  • Is there an action I am postponing because I want a guarantee that nobody can give?
  • What information, rest or support would help me choose more carefully?
  • Where am I asking an individual to solve a problem that requires a change in shared conditions?

An optional practice: Identify one manageable step toward something important. Before taking it, consider who might be affected and what support you need. Afterwards, assess the care of your decision separately from its outcome.
